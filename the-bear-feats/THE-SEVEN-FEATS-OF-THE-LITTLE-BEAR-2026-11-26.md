## Introduction

The hero of this book is about seven centimetres long. He is a bear carved from jet — fossilised wood, light and warm in the hand, polished to a gloss — in the Zuni carving tradition of the American Southwest. A heartline of red coral, fitted in segments into a cut channel, runs from his mouth along his flank to an arrowhead over his heart: the breath of life entering, and carried to the heart. His one visible eye is a disc of turquoise with a small dark centre. He carries no maker's name, as the older work usually does not; in that tradition the style itself was the signature. In the six-directions set recorded by Frank Hamilton Cushing in 1883, the bear is Guardian and Master of the West, and across the Southwest he carries strength and healing. His age cannot be proved from photographs, but the best judgment places him in the 1960s or 1970s, when jet heartline bears were carved in numbers. How he travelled from the Southwest to Kansas is not recorded. What is recorded is his present keeping: he rides in Robert Tilford's pocket, he sat for a time on top of the refrigerator as a blessing over the food, and since the twenty-sixth of November 2026 he rests beside Robert's books, kept dry and out of the sun, with one small item waiting for an artisan — the seam around his arrowhead has opened slightly, and there is a chip of about a millimetre at its tip. In Robert's object inventory, begun that same week at his direction, the bear is catalogued as No. 003; No. 001 is a stirrup, and No. 002 the amber specimens.

The feats were all performed on one day: Thanksgiving Day, the twenty-sixth of November 2026. The morning's work had included the bear's cataloguing and a conversation about the writer Alan Moore, who had spoken in an interview of a novel written without a letter. In the course of that talk it came out — to Robert's surprise, and he said so — that a small piece about the bear, written at the desk that day, contained no letter D at all, and that he had read it without noticing. A fence he had not seen had been standing around the words the whole time. That discovery opened the game. If one letter could be banished without the reader noticing, what else could be done? By the end of the day, the little bear had been put through seven distinct feats of constrained writing, and this book is their record and their explanation.

Constrained writing, stated plainly, is writing under a vow. The writer gives up something the language normally supplies — a letter, a vowel, a stock of words, the freedom to let a sentence run to its natural length — and then must tell the story anyway, with whatever the vow leaves standing. The vow is not decoration. It is a fence, and everything interesting in these pieces happens because the fence was there: words had to be invented, names had to be surrendered and replaced, sentences had to climb stairs built out of arithmetic. The tradition is an honourable one, and this book names its ancestors in every chapter and gives them their credit, because the feats here stand in a line that runs from an ancient psalm through a French novelist who wrote a whole book without the commonest letter in his language to a children's author who won a bet with fifty words.

One house rule governs everything that follows, and it is stated here so that no reader has to take anything on trust. Every claim in this book carries its count with it. Where a chapter says a letter appears zero times, the count was made by machine against the finished text, and the method is given. Where a chapter says a piece is the first of its kind that the desk could find, it says exactly that and no more: none found in the searches run, which is a statement about the searches, not a census of the world. The closing chapter explains every counting method in plain language, so that any reader with patience can recheck every figure in the book. The desk would rather be checked than believed.

The seven feats are these. First, a telling of the bear with the letter D banished entirely. Second, a poem whose margin spells a maxim down its side. Third, a story whose alphabet dies letter by letter as it proceeds. Fourth, the bear's whole story told with exactly fifty distinct words. Fifth, a story whose sentences climb the Fibonacci staircase. Sixth, that same staircase climbed again with two letters — A and D together — banished at once. Seventh, a story in five movements in which the vowels die one at a time until only E remains. Each chapter gives the piece in full, then states its rule, then shows what the rule forced, then gives the proof, then names the ancestors. The bear comes first and last in all of it; the arithmetic is only ever in his service.

## Chapter One — The D-Less Bear

The bear is small, black, smooth in a palm. He is jet, a tree grown patient in earth until time turn him to stone-light coal. A Zuni carver set his eye with turquoise, sky-blue, plus a heartline in coral from mouth to heart — breath walking home. Coral is sea-stuff, its hue the hue of life in veins. In Zuni country the bear keeps the West; he is strength, healing, shelter for whoever carries him. No name marks his feet; his style signs him. He was lost once, or given away, or simply left where houses empty. Now he sits in Kansas, by the books of a man who rubs his flank, keeps him from sun, waits for the right artisan to seal one tiny seam.

### The Rule

The vow of this piece is a lipogram in the letter D. The letter D appears nowhere in it, in either case, and because the word "and" contains that letter, the commonest joining word in English is gone with it. Everything else in the language was allowed; that one letter, and every word built on it, was not.

### What the Rule Forced

The ban reached further into the story than a first glance suggests. The piece could not say "red," though the heartline is red. It could not say "hand," though the bear lives in one. It could not say "sacred" or "wood," though jet is fossilised wood and the carving tradition is a sacred one. So the piece invented its way around the fence. Jet became "stone-light coal," a tree grown patient in the earth until time turned it — the piece's grammar bends there too, "until time turn him," because the ordinary past-tense forms it reached for kept arriving with the banned letter on their backs. Coral became "sea-stuff," named by where it comes from rather than by its name. The heartline, which could not be described with half its usual vocabulary, became "breath walking home." These inventions are not ornaments added afterward; they are the shapes the story took when its first-choice words were refused.

### The Proof

The figures of record are these: the piece runs to 126 words, and the letter D appears in it zero times, counted by machine before the piece was first delivered. One circumstance of its keeping deserves plain statement. The piece was first delivered in conversation and was not saved as a file at the time; the text printed above was afterward recovered verbatim from the record of that delivery, and on recovery the word count and the letter census were run again against the recovered text, with the same results.

### The Ancestors

The lipogram is the oldest device in this book, and its modern masters are two. Ernest Vincent Wright published *Gadsby* in 1939, a novel of more than fifty thousand words written without the letter E; he wrote it, by his own account, because he kept hearing that such a thing could not be done, and he tied down the E key of his typewriter so the letter could not slip in. Georges Perec followed in French with *La Disparition* in 1969, likewise without E, carried into English by Gilbert Adair as *A Void*. In our own century Alice Lyons wrote *Oona*, a novel without the letter O. The D-less bear stands in their line at a humbler length: one page instead of a novel, and a quieter letter — but the discovery that started this whole book was theirs before it was ours, the discovery that a reader can walk through a text with a letter missing and never feel the gap.

## Chapter Two — The Maxim Acrostic

Little bear of black jet, carved in the Zuni way,
Out of dark stone he came, small enough for a pocket,
Veined with a heartline of coral, red as a thread of life,
Ending at an arrowhead laid along his flank.
In one eye a fleck of turquoise keeps its watch,
Set there by hands that signed no name.
The West is his country, in the old reckoning —
He is its Guardian, its Master, bearer of strength and healing.
Every day he rides along in a man's pocket,
Patient under the rub of a thumb, smooth with handling.
Once he stood over the kitchen from the top of the refrigerator,
Watching the food below like a blessing with four small feet.
Evenings now he rests beside the books, out of the sun,
Resting in Hays, in Kansas, far from the desert that dreamed him.
Someday the road may take him back to New Mexico,
Under an artisan's lamp, to be looked at and known.
Pocket-worn and palm-warmed, he asks for nothing,
Rubbed smooth by one man's hand, kept close as a promise.
Even a stone can travel far when it is carried kindly.
Medicine bear, heartline bear, bear of the red thread —
Endure with us a while; the road is better with you on it.

### The Rule

The poem is an acrostic. The first letter of each successive line, read downward along the margin, spells Robert Tilford's maxim: LOVE IS THE POWER SUPREME. The maxim has twenty-one letters, so the poem has twenty-one lines, and every line's opening word was chosen under orders from the letter that stood at its gate. The poem was also required to stand as a plain poem about the bear for any reader who never looks at the margin; the hidden frame was not allowed to bend the visible one out of shape.

### What the Rule Forced

A margin that spells a sentence is a stern editor. The third line had to begin with V, and the heartline's description arrived as "Veined with a heartline of coral" — a word the poem might not have found on its own, and a good one. The refrigerator blessing, one of the fixed facts of the bear's life, had to be carried by a line beginning with O, and so it entered as "Once he stood over the kitchen," a storyteller's opening the poem is better for. The hope of New Mexico had to pass through gates lettered S and U, and came out as "Someday the road may take him back" and "Under an artisan's lamp." Near the end the maxim demanded P, R, E, M, E in succession, and the poem answered with "Pocket-worn and palm-warmed," "Rubbed smooth by one man's hand," "Even a stone can travel far," "Medicine bear, heartline bear," and the closing "Endure with us a while." Again and again the letter at the gate chose the word, and the word chose the thought.

### The Proof

The check was made by a script that read the poem's lines from the saved file, took the first alphabetic character of each line, uppercased the letters, and joined them into one string for comparison against the maxim. The joined string is LOVEISTHEPOWERSUPREME. The line count is twenty-one. The string matches the maxim exactly, letter for letter, with no remainder and no shortfall.

### The Ancestors

The acrostic is the one device in this book whose ancestor is not a modern experimenter but an ancient text. Psalm 119, the longest chapter of the Bible, is an alphabetic acrostic: its stanzas proceed through the letters of the Hebrew alphabet in order, each stanza's lines beginning with the letter that governs it. The device was already old when it was used there — a way of saying that a subject had been treated completely, from the first letter to the last, with nothing left outside the frame. The bear's poem borrows that old architecture for a new sentence: not the alphabet this time, but a maxim, carried down the margin the way the psalm carries its letters, complete from L to E with nothing left out.

## Chapter Three — The Vanishing Alphabet

*The frame: this story is told in eight paragraphs, and the alphabet dies as it goes. The first paragraph uses all twenty-six letters. Then one letter dies at each step, in the order Z, Q, J, X, V, K, W, and is never used again — so the language tightens, paragraph by paragraph, all the way down.*

The bear began in the far west, carved from black jet by Zuni hands: a quick, quiet guardian no bigger than a thumb, with a red coral heartline running from his mouth along his flank to an arrowhead, and one turquoise eye. Packed in a box of soft cloth, he crossed plains and rivers on a long ride, exactly the sort of small, brave companion a man might keep — and he came at last to Hays, Kansas, and to Robert.

In the old teaching of the six directions, the bear stands at the west, guardian and master of that quarter, and his gifts are strength and healing. Robert learned this and liked the bear the more for it. A guardian you can hold in one hand, he thought, is a fine sort of guardian for a small apartment and a long life.

He rode in Robert's pocket most days, warm from the hand that held him, and in the evenings he sat on top of the refrigerator, a small blessing over the food of the house. Robert rubbed him in his palm when he prayed, the way a man turns a smooth stone, and the bear took the shine of it.

The bear is black stone, seven centimeters from nose to tail, light as a secret in the palm. The red line runs true along his side and ends in the arrowhead, and his one blue-green eye has seen the inside of a pocket, the top of a refrigerator, and the lamplight of a Kansas evening.

In time Robert moved him off the refrigerator and set him beside his books, out of the sun, in a place of honor among the stories. The bear did not mind. A guardian is patient, and stone is the most patient thing of all; he kept his post by the books the way he had kept it over the food.

Some mornings Robert lifts him and holds him to the light, and the red line along his side burns like a coal. The bear says nothing. He has said nothing in all his years, and still Robert hears him: be strong, be calm, bear up, and heal.

He rests now beside the stories Robert has made, in Hays, on a plain shelf in a small room. The sun does not find him there. He is safe from heat and harm, and his red heartline holds its fire in the shade, patient as stone, faithful as a dog that cannot stray.

One day, if the road is good, Robert means to carry him south to the land he came from, and set him in an artisan's hands, and say: see the little seam at the tip, mend it if you can. Until that day the bear abides in Hays, a small guardian at rest, his heartline red as a coal in the lamplight, his silence a blessing on the house.

### The Rule

The story is told in eight paragraphs, and the alphabet dies as it goes. The first paragraph must use all twenty-six letters. Then one letter dies at each step, in the order Z, Q, J, X, V, K, and W, and a dead letter is never used again. The ban is cumulative: the second paragraph writes without Z; the third without Z and Q; and so on down to the eighth, which writes with seven letters buried. The language available to the story tightens paragraph by paragraph, while the story itself is required to proceed as if nothing were wrong.

### What the Rule Forced

The deaths were chosen to take the expendable letters first — Z goes in the opening step, and the first paragraph spends it while it still can on "Zuni" itself — but the tightening is real long before the end. The most concrete casualty stands in the last paragraph and is confessed in the proof below: the word "arrowhead," one of the fixed furniture words of the bear's story, carries a W, and W is the last letter to die, so in the final paragraph the arrowhead could not be named at all. The bear's one known injury had to be carried instead as "the little seam at the tip," and the sentence that sends him south to the artisan is built around that substitution. The same paragraph cannot say "west," though west is the bear's own country, and must send him instead "south to the land he came from." Paragraph by paragraph the story keeps its facts and loses its favourite words for them, which is precisely the sensation the piece exists to give: a world intact, a vocabulary quietly narrowing like a road.

### The Proof

The check was made by a script that isolated the eight story paragraphs — the title, the label, the frame line, and the file's own record standing outside the count — computed the set of letters from A to Z used in each paragraph, and tested the first paragraph for completeness and each later paragraph against the cumulative list of the dead. Every paragraph passed. The first paragraph uses all twenty-six letters, none missing. The second uses twenty-three letters and no Z. The third uses twenty-two, with neither Z nor Q. The fourth uses twenty-two, with Z, Q, and J all absent. The fifth uses twenty-two, with X now dead as well. The sixth uses twenty letters, V having joined the dead. The seventh uses twenty, with K also gone. The eighth uses nineteen letters, and none of Z, Q, J, X, V, K, or W appears in it. One repair was made during the checking and is disclosed here rather than tidied away: the eighth paragraph first carried the word "arrowhead," whose W broke the final ban; it now reads "the little seam at the tip," and after the repair the full check was run again, and all eight paragraphs passed.

### The Ancestors

Two ancestors govern this chapter, one for each direction of its motion. Walter Abish published *Alphabetical Africa* in 1974, a novel whose early chapters unlock the alphabet letter by letter — the first chapter confined to words beginning with A, the next admitting B, and so on — until the whole alphabet stands open, after which the chapters lock back down again in reverse. The vanishing bear story is Abish's road walked in the opposite direction and by subtraction rather than admission: not words rationed by their first letters, but letters themselves struck from the whole vocabulary, one funeral at a time. The second ancestor is Mark Dunn's *Ella Minnow Pea* of 2001, a novel in letters in which an island council bans letters of the alphabet one by one by law, and the book's own language loses them as the decrees arrive, so that the constraint is not a frame around the story but an event inside it. The bear's story borrows that inwardness: the deaths are not announced by any council, but they happen to the telling itself, and the reader who watches the words narrow is watching the same slow emergency Dunn put on his island.

## Chapter Four — The Fifty-Word Bear

A Zuni hand carved the little bear from black jet. The bear is black from the jet, and the jet is black in the hand.

A red coral heartline runs from his mouth over his flank to the arrowhead. The heartline is red, and the arrowhead is red, and his eye is turquoise.

The bear is guardian of the west. The bear is master of the west. He is strength. He is healing. He is a blessing in the hand.

The bear came along on a ride. The bear came on the road to Hays. The bear came to Kansas, and Kansas is home now.

Robert keeps the bear in his pocket. Robert rubs the bear in his hand. He rubs and rubs the little bear, and the bear keeps Robert.

Robert keeps the bear on the refrigerator. The bear is a blessing over the food.

Now Robert keeps the bear beside the books. The bear is beside the books, and the bear is in the pocket, and the bear is in the hand. The bear is home. Hays is home. Kansas is home. The little black bear from the west is home.

### The Rule

The vow here is not about letters but about stock. The whole story — the carving, the heartline, the guardianship, the journey, the pocket, the refrigerator, the books — must be told using exactly fifty distinct words. Words may be repeated as often as the story needs; the fence stands around the vocabulary, not around the telling. Fifty words, no more, and the fifty-first word, however useful, stays outside.

### What the Rule Forced

A vocabulary of fifty words cannot afford synonyms, so repetition becomes the piece's music and its meaning. The verb "keeps" must do nearly all the work of possession and care: Robert keeps the bear in his pocket, keeps him on the refrigerator, keeps him beside the books. Then, in the middle of the story, the word turns around — "He rubs and rubs the little bear, and the bear keeps Robert" — and the repetition the rule forced becomes the truest sentence in the piece. The ending is built the same way, out of the single word "home" struck again and again like a bell: "The bear is home. Hays is home. Kansas is home." Nothing in the fifty words is wasted. "Refrigerator" earns its place as the one long word, the blessing over the food could not be told without it, and the story's whole geography — Hays, Kansas, the west — had to fit inside the same small stock as "the" and "is" and "a."

### The Proof

The count was made mechanically from the story text, with the words reduced to lowercase and their punctuation stripped before counting. The number of distinct words is exactly fifty. The total number of words in the telling is one hundred ninety-three — the fifty, repeated as needed. The fifty words themselves, in alphabetical order, are these: a, along, and, arrowhead, bear, beside, black, blessing, books, came, carved, coral, eye, flank, food, from, guardian, hand, hays, he, healing, heartline, his, home, in, is, jet, kansas, keeps, little, master, mouth, now, of, on, over, pocket, red, refrigerator, ride, road, robert, rubs, runs, strength, the, to, turquoise, west, zuni. Any reader may count them against the story above; the list and the story are the same size, and neither has anything hidden in it.

### The Ancestors

This chapter has one ancestor, and the debt is total and gladly owned. In 1960 Dr. Seuss published *Green Eggs and Ham*, written on a bet with his publisher, Bennett Cerf, that he could not write a book using only fifty distinct words. He won the bet, and the book became one of the best-loved children's books in the language, its tiny vocabulary carried entirely by rhythm, repetition, and nerve. The fifty-word bear takes Seuss's exact fence — the same number, the same method of repetition without apology — and walks a true story through it instead of a comic one: a real carving, a real journey, a real man's keeping. Where the ancestor proved that fifty words are enough for delight, this chapter tries the same fifty-word measure against affection and fact, and finds, as he did, that the fence is not a shortage. It is an engine.

## Chapter Five — The Fibonacci Bear

Jet. Carved. Turquoise eye. Coral heartline runs. From mouth to arrowhead, red. Seven centimetres long, small guardian of the West. He rode along to Hays in Kansas heat, in a kind stranger's keeping. Robert keeps him in his pocket by day and rubs him in his hand for strength and healing and quiet comfort. Once he blessed the food from the refrigerator top; now he rests beside the books, out of the sun, where Robert's hand still finds him each morning and thanks him for coming safely home.

### The Rule

The story is told in exactly nine sentences, and the sentences must climb a staircase. The first sentence has one word. The second has one word. The third has two, the fourth three, the fifth five, the sixth eight, the seventh thirteen, the eighth twenty-one, and the ninth thirty-four — the Fibonacci sequence, in which every step is the sum of the two steps before it. No sentence may be padded to reach its number and none may be trimmed below it; each must be a true sentence of the bear's story that happens to be exactly as long as the staircase demands.

### What the Rule Forced

At the foot of the stairs, the rule forces the story down to its elements, and the elements turn out to be the bear himself: "Jet." "Carved." One word for the material, one for the making, and nothing else fits. The third step can afford exactly two words, and the story spends them on the eye — "Turquoise eye" — because the eye is the bear's one spot of colour against the black. As the steps widen, the facts arrive in the order the arithmetic allows: the heartline at five words, his size and guardianship at eight, the journey at thirteen, the daily keeping at twenty-one. The summit is the test of the whole design. A sentence of thirty-four words must not sprawl; it must be one held breath. The story spends it on the full arc of the bear's rest — the refrigerator top behind him, the books beside him, the sun kept off him, and Robert's hand finding him each morning to thank him for coming safely home — so that the longest sentence is also the warmest, and the staircase ends not in arithmetic but in gratitude.

### The Proof

The counting was done mechanically, the sentences split on their terminal punctuation and the words counted as the separate tokens between spaces. The per-sentence word counts are 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, and 34, matching the sequence exactly, step for step, with no step missed and no step repeated. The total is eighty-eight words. The check was run against the saved text of the piece on the day it was made.

### The Ancestors

The staircase has two modern keepers. Gregory K. Pincus brought the form to wide attention on the first of April 2006 with the Fib: a poem of six lines whose syllable counts follow the sequence — 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 — twenty syllables in all, a haiku's stricter cousin. The bear's staircase differs from the Fib in two ways: it counts words rather than syllables, and it climbs three steps higher, past Pincus's landing at 8 to 13, 21, and 34, because a story needs more room than a lyric. The second keeper is Bruce Holland Rogers, whose Fibonacci Sonnet is a two-paragraph prose form in which the word counts of the sentences follow the sequence up and then back down, the story swelling and subsiding like a breath. The bear's telling borrows Rogers's prose insight — that sentence lengths, not line lengths, can carry the staircase — but climbs in one direction only, up, and stops at the summit, because the bear's story is a journey toward rest and does not come back down.

## Chapter Six — The Bruin's Staircase

Jet. Bruin. Turquoise eye. Rose line runs. From mouth to one point. He is the West's keeper: strength, quiet help. He went on one long journey from the Zuni hills to Robert's town. Robert keeps him in one pocket, rubs him with his thumb; once he kept blessing over the house's suppers up high. Now he rests by the books, out of the sun, in Robert's keeping, the rose line quiet in evening light, till one spring Robert brings him home to the stone folk of New Mexico.

### The Rule

This chapter stands under three vows at once, and it is the only piece in the book that stacks its fences. The first vow is the staircase of the previous chapter, unchanged: exactly nine sentences, of 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, and 34 words. The second vow is a double ban: the letters A and D appear nowhere in the story, in either case — two letters gone together, where the first chapter of this book banished only one. The third vow is fidelity: under both restrictions, the story must still be whole and true, carrying the bear's full arc from the Zuni hills to the bookshelf, with the hope of New Mexico at the top of the stairs.

### What the Rule Forced

The double ban took the story's own names from it, and the renamings are the chapter's signature. The hero could not be called a bear at all — the word "bear" carries the first banned letter — so he travels under the old word "bruin," and the piece takes its title from his exile. The heartline could not be red, and could not be a heartline, and its coral could not be named either; it became the "rose line," a name the ban invented and the story is prouder of than the one it lost. Healing, a banned word, arrived as "quiet help." Food, banned, became "suppers." Hays and Kansas, both banned, dissolved into "Robert's town," and the refrigerator, banned several times over, survives only as the height from which the blessing was kept — "up high." Even the grammar had to emigrate: with "and" gone and the past tense mostly closed, the sentences lean on semicolons and on the simple present, and the one long journey is told in a single clean stride. What is remarkable, reading it, is how little is missing. The bear arrives entire; only his vocabulary was left at the border.

### The Proof

Both vows were checked mechanically against the story text. The sentences were split on their terminal punctuation and their words counted as the tokens between spaces: the counts are 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, and 34, an exact match to the staircase, and the total is eighty-eight words — the same total as the previous chapter, reached this time with two letters missing. A letter census of the whole story text, counted without regard to case, returns the two figures on which the chapter stands: A equals zero, and D equals zero. Not one slip of either letter anywhere in the eighty-eight words.

### The Ancestors

This chapter has four ancestors, two for each of its fences, and it names them in pairs. For the ban, the masters are Ernest Vincent Wright and Georges Perec: Wright, whose *Gadsby* of 1939 banished E from a whole novel because he kept hearing it could not be done, his E key tied down against slips; and Perec, whose *La Disparition* of 1969 did the same in French and reached English as *A Void* in Gilbert Adair's translation. Each of them banished one letter, and the one they chose was the commonest in its language. The bruin's staircase banishes two letters at once, but at a story's length rather than a novel's, and the book states the trade plainly rather than pretending the feats are the same size. For the staircase, the ancestors are Gregory K. Pincus, whose Fib of 2006 counts syllables up the first six steps, and Bruce Holland Rogers, whose Fibonacci Sonnet counts words per sentence up the stairs and back down. The stack itself — staircase and double ban standing on one small text at the same time — is this house's own contribution, and the closing chapter states exactly how far that claim extends and no further.

## Chapter Seven — The Five Deaths of the Vowels

A story in five movements. The vowels die one at a time — first U, then O, then I, then A — until only E is left, and the bear rests in the one sound that remains.

### 1

A hand carved the little bear from black jet, in the Zuni way, and set a heartline of red coral running from his mouth along his flank to an arrowhead. One eye is turquoise, bright as a bead of summer sky. In the six directions of his people, the bear stands at the West: Guardian and Master of that quarter, a bearer of strength and of healing. He is small, seven centimetres from nose to tail, and unsigned, as such carvings are — the maker trusted the stone to speak.

### 2

Then came the long ride east. Packed in soft cloth, in a box, the bear crossed plains and rivers, past fields and small towns, sleeping in the dark as the miles fell behind him. At last the ride ended in Hays, in Kansas; the box opened in a small apartment, and Robert lifted him into lamplight. The traveling was done. The bear had his man, and his man had him.

### 3

Days settled in a rhythm. The man carried him daily, in a denim nest at his hip, and in idle times his palm pressed the little bear, again and again, till the jet gleamed. At mealtimes the bear sat high in the kitchen, at the fridge's white rim, a silent blessing, watching the bread and the milk and the cheese.

### 4

Then the man gave the bear a new place. The bear rested deep between the tales, where the day's harsh glare never reached. A shelf near the lamp became the bear's haven: safe, shaded, calm. At evenfall the lamp gleamed, and the red thread that ran the flank's length held the warmth. The man's hand went back day after day, and rested near the bear, and the bear kept the same blessed watch, as ever.

### 5

He rests here, where the shelves keep the texts. The jet emblem sleeps, serene, blessed, the West's keeper. Strength rests here yet. He never left; even the deep rest keeps the emblem, ever.

### The Rule

The story is told in five movements, and the vowels die one at a time as it proceeds. The vowels are exactly five — A, E, I, O, U — and the letter Y is treated throughout as a consonant. The first movement is full life: ordinary English, using all five vowels. Then the deaths begin, in an order chosen by rarity, the least-needed vowel dying first. In the second movement U is dead and appears nowhere. In the third, O is dead too. In the fourth, I has joined them, and only A and E remain. In the fifth movement A dies last, and E is the only vowel left alive in the text. The story must go on being the bear's story the whole way down.

### What the Rule Forced

Each death took a household word with it, and the story survived by renaming, as it had all day. When U died, the bear lost his people's name — "Zuni" could no longer be written — and his title "Guardian" with it, so the second movement carries him east in silence about his origins, a traveller whose papers were left behind. When O died, the casualties were intimate: "Robert" itself became unwritable, and the man of the story is "the man" from that movement on; "pocket" died, and the bear rides instead in "a denim nest at his hip"; "food" died, and the blessing in the kitchen watches over "the bread and the milk and the cheese," the stores named one by one because the word for all of them was gone. When I died, the books became "the tales," the evening became "evenfall," and lamplight had to be carried by "the lamp gleamed." And in the last movement, with A dead, even "the bear" could no longer be written — the word carries an A — so the hero comes to rest under the one clean name the language had left for him: "the jet emblem." The final movement is four sentences long, because that is about as much world as one vowel can hold, and it lands, as it was meant to, like a held breath.

### The Proof

The check was made by a script that read the five movement paragraphs from the saved file and computed, for each, the set of vowels present in it, counting without regard to case and treating Y as a consonant, and tested each set against the rule for its movement. All five movements passed. The first movement's vowel set is the full company: A, E, I, O, and U. The second movement's set is A, E, I, and O — U absent. The third's is A, E, and I — U and O both absent. The fourth's is A and E alone. The fifth movement's set is a single letter: E. Two repairs were made under the check and are disclosed here. The third movement first read "refrigerator's," which carries an O, and now reads "the fridge's." The fourth movement first carried the word "of" in the phrase "the length of the flank," and now reads "the flank's length." After the repairs the full check was run again against the saved file, and all five movements passed again.

### The Ancestors

Three ancestors stand over this chapter. The first is Georges Perec again, this time for the mirror image of his famous ban: in *Les Revenentes* of 1972 he wrote a work in which E is the only vowel used at all — where *La Disparition* exiles one letter, *Les Revenentes* admits one vowel and no other, from the first page to the last. The fifth movement of the bear's story is a small room built to Perec's plan; the difference is that the bear arrives there by a road, descending through the deaths, where Perec simply begins inside the room and never leaves it. The second ancestor is Christian Bök, whose *Eunoia* of 2001 devotes each of its chapters to a single vowel — one chapter written in A alone, one in E, one in I, one in O, one in U — proving at book length that any one vowel, held faithfully, can carry a voice. The third is Mark Dunn, whose *Ella Minnow Pea* made letter-death a story's plot; the bear's descent borrows Dunn's inwardness but applies the dying to the vowels only, and in a fixed order of rarity, so that the language does not merely shrink but thins in a particular direction — toward E, the commonest vowel in English, the last sound a story can make before silence.

## Closing Chapter — How to Check Us

Everything in this book can be checked by its readers, and this chapter exists so that the checking needs no one's permission and no special equipment. The methods are few and plain.

To count the words of a sentence, take the sentence by itself and count the separate tokens standing between spaces, treating a word with its punctuation attached as one word: "Jet." is one word, "heartline" is one word, and a sentence's count is simply the number of such tokens between its beginning and its full stop. That is the whole method behind both staircase chapters, and any reader who counts the nine sentences of either story on their fingers will arrive at 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, or will have found an error the desk wants to hear about.

To make a distinct-word census, as the fifty-word chapter requires, go through the piece and write down each word the first time it appears, ignoring capital letters and stripping punctuation, so that "The" at the head of a sentence and "the" in its middle are one word, and "home." at a sentence's end is the same word as "home" anywhere else. When the piece is finished, count the list. If the list holds fifty words, the vow held; the story's total length, counted token by token as above, is a separate and larger number, and the book has kept the two counts distinct wherever both are given.

To make a letter census, choose the letter and count every appearance of it in the text, in either case, in every word, including the ones hiding inside other words — the D inside "and," the A inside "bear." A census is a complete count of a defined set: nothing sampled, nothing estimated. That completeness is what makes a zero worth having. A letter census that returns zero is not an impression that the letter is rare; it is a proof that it is absent, of the same kind as the vowel census used in the final chapter, where the set counted is the five vowels A, E, I, O, and U — with the letter Y counted as a consonant throughout, a convention this book states openly wherever it matters, since a checker who used the other convention would be counting a different set and might reach a different figure honestly.

To check the acrostic, no counting is needed at all: read the first letter of each line of the poem in the second chapter, downward, in order, and write the letters in a row. They spell the maxim, and the check is finished in under a minute.

Two claims in this book are claims of novelty, and they are restated here in exactly the bounded form in which the desk is willing to stand behind them. The first concerns the Bruin's Staircase: that a Fibonacci sentence staircase and a double ban on the letters A and D should stand together on one single text is, in the desk's searches of 2026-11-26 across the lipogram, double-lipogram, and Fibonacci shelves, none found in the searches run. The second concerns the Five Deaths of the Vowels: that a story should descend through the vowels one death at a time and end in a single-vowel movement is likewise none found in the searches run on 2026-11-26. Neither claim is stated anywhere in this book as "first ever," and no reader should repeat them in that form. A search finds what it finds; it does not empty the world. If a predecessor surfaces, the claim falls, and the pieces lose nothing but a sentence in this chapter — the counts, which are the book's real substance, are untouched by the question of who else may have climbed the same stairs.

Finally, a note about a second pair of eyes. On 2026-11-26 an independent recount of the seven feats was commissioned from SpokBot of the Grok crew — a checker that built none of the pieces and owes the desk nothing. Its verdict, whatever it finds, whether it confirms every figure or catches the desk in an error, will be entered in this book's record by dated addendum, in the open, where any reader can see it. The desk counts its own work and stands behind the counts; it also prefers, on principle, not to be the only counter in the room.

## Appendix — How It Was Done: The D-Less Bear Taken Apart

This appendix exists for one particular reader: the one who finishes the first chapter of this book, looks up, and says — what? No D's? How is that possible? It is a fair question, and it deserves more than a shrug and an invitation to trust the desk. So here the smallest and plainest of the seven feats is taken apart, slowly, to show that there is no trick in it anywhere: only a ban, a method, and a count. What is true of this piece is true, with different vows, of all seven.

### The Reader's Moment

Begin by checking the piece yourself, with nothing but your eyes and a pencil. First, read it once for pleasure, the way it was meant to be read — a small black bear, a turquoise eye, a heartline, a man in Kansas. Nothing announces itself. That is the first fact of the lipogram, and it is worth sitting with for a moment: a text can be missing one of the commonest letters in the language, and a reader can walk through it from end to end and feel no gap at all.

Then take a copy of the text — print it, or copy it into a fresh page — and run a letter census by hand. Read through it slowly, word by word, and put a tally mark on your paper every time you meet the letter D, in either case, standing alone or hiding inside a word. Take your time; the piece is only 126 words. When you reach the end, count your tally marks. There are none. The tally comes out zero, and a zero you counted yourself is a proof you own — not a figure the desk reported to you, but a fact your own hand established. That difference matters, and it is the whole spirit of this book.

With the census done, look once more at what else is missing, because the ban's shadow is longer than the letter itself. The word "and" is nowhere in the piece — it cannot be, since it is built around the banned letter — and English without "and" is English with its commonest joining word removed. Notice what the sentences do instead. They lean on commas and semicolons; they place their clauses side by side and let the reader feel the joins. "He is jet, a tree grown patient in earth until time turn him to stone-light coal." The sentence holds together the way a dry-stone wall holds together: by placement, not by mortar. Once you have seen the missing "and," you can never quite unsee it, and the piece reads differently ever after — which is the reader's real initiation into this game. The constraint was never hidden, exactly. It was only unannounced.

### How It Was Built

The working method has three parts, and none of them is mysterious. The first is to list, before drafting, what the ban kills. For the letter D the casualty list runs: "and" itself; every regular past tense formed with "-ed," which is most of the past tense of English; and a crowd of everyday words the story would normally reach for without thinking — "would," "could," "good," "hand," "red," "world," "carved." A builder who knows the casualty list in advance does not waste grief on each word as it falls; the list is the map of the closed roads, drawn before the journey starts.

The second part is the three tools that carry the builder through the open country. The first tool is the present tense, which walks around the closed past tense entirely: the bear does not "sat" or "rested" in this piece — he sits, he keeps, he waits, and the telling gains a stillness that suits a stone bear anyway. The second tool is the synonym hunt, the patient search for the word that means nearly the same and is built from permitted letters: "small" for the banned "little"? No — "little" is permitted and "small" is too, but "smooth" must carry what "soft" and "polished" could not both say, and every sentence holds two or three such quiet substitutions. The third tool is the one that turns the whole exercise from evasion into writing: circumlocution that deepens into imagery. Jet had to be described without "wood" and its kin, and the way around — "a tree grown patient in earth until time turn him to stone-light coal" — is not a dodge but a better sentence than the plain one would have been, because it tells the truth about what jet actually is. Coral, unnamed, became "sea-stuff, its hue the hue of life in veins," which is again simply accurate, said from the side because the front door was locked. This is the secret the appendix exists to tell: the ban does not merely forbid. It forces the writer off the main road, and some of the country out there is finer than the road was.

The third part of the method is the loop, and it is the least romantic and the most important. Draft loosely, without terror of slips. Then count mechanically — every D, wherever it hides. Then repair the slips the count exposes, one by one, finding for each a permitted way to say the thing. Then count again. Repeat until the census reads zero. The loop, and not any feat of vigilance during drafting, is what produces a clean text; no builder holds a whole piece in his head letter by letter, and none of the seven feats in this book was made by anyone who did. Each was made by drafting, counting, repairing, and counting again — the same loop, turned on seven different vows: a letter census for the bans, a token count for the staircases, a distinct-word census for the fifty words, a vowel census for the deaths, a reading down the margin for the acrostic. Learn the loop and you have learned the whole workshop.

### The Doorway into the Mathematics

It may surprise a reader who came for a bear to be told that he has been doing mathematics. He has, and of a respectable kind. A census — a complete count of every member of a defined set — is the same act whether the set is the letter D in a paragraph of 126 words or the verses in a book of thousands: define the set exactly, count all of it, estimate none of it. The closing chapter of this book leans on that identity deliberately. A translation desk that counts verses for a living and a poet counting letters are using one instrument at two sizes, and the instrument's virtue is the same at both: the result does not depend on who is holding it.

Zero deserves a word of its own, because laymen distrust it. A count that comes out zero feels like a failure to find rather than a finding. It is the opposite. Zero, reached by a complete census, is a provable answer — as solid as any number on the page, and in the lipogram's case it is the entire claim. The D-less bear does not say that D is rare in it, or scarce, or avoided where possible. It says the census is zero, and the census can be re-run by anyone, including the reader with the pencil described above, and it will come out zero every time. That is what proof feels like at this scale: not an argument, but a recount that refuses to change its answer.

And the staircase chapters are the same game played with numbers instead of letters. There the defined set is the words in a sentence, and the pattern they must follow is the sequence in which each number is the sum of the two before it — 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34 — a sequence set out by Leonardo of Pisa, called Fibonacci, in his *Liber Abaci* of 1202, where it described, among other things, the growth of a population of rabbits. Eight centuries later it describes the growth of a story about a bear, one sentence at a time, from a single word of black jet to a sentence long enough to hold a homecoming. Letters or numbers, the game is one game: fix a rule that can be checked, build under it honestly, and let the count say whether you succeeded.

### Who Invented This Game

No one did, and the credit has to be given in that honest, scattered form. The earliest lipogram on record is credited to Lasus of Hermione, a Greek poet of the sixth century before Christ, whose hymn avoided the letter sigma — he is said to have disliked its hissing sound — so the game is older than most of the literature that survives from its world. It was played occasionally across the centuries that followed, a curiosity passed hand to hand, until the modern age gave it something it had never had: a workshop.

The workshop was founded in Paris in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais, who named it the Oulipo — a name usually rendered as the Workshop of Potential Literature. Its founding principle is the one this whole book runs on: that a constraint is not a cage but a machine for forcing new decisions, and that a writer who gives something up on purpose will invent things a writer with everything available would never have found. The stone-light coal in the first chapter is that principle at work in miniature, twenty-six centuries after Lasus and sixty-six years after the workshop opened.

Its two great modern masters, named here with their books because they earned the naming, are Ernest Vincent Wright and Georges Perec. Wright, an American, published *Gadsby* in 1939 — a full novel without the letter E, written because he kept hearing the thing could not be done, his E key tied down so the letter could not betray him in an unguarded moment. Perec, a member of the Oulipo, published *La Disparition* in 1969, the same feat in French, carried into English by Gilbert Adair as *A Void*; he also wrote the one-vowel mirror of it, *Les Revenentes*, in which E is the only vowel admitted. Between Wright's typewriter key and Perec's workshop, the game became what it is now: not a stunt but a discipline, with a literature of its own.

The appendix closes, as it should, with the door held open. Everything in this book was done with a ban, a loop, and a count, and none of it requires anything a reader does not already own. So the dare, offered kindly and in earnest: choose one letter, write one paragraph under its ban — about anything, a cat, a kitchen, a grandfather — and then count it, by hand, and repair what the count finds, and count again until the census reads zero. The paragraph will fight you in instructive places. It will also hand you at least one sentence you would never have written without the fence, and you will know, from the inside, exactly how the little bear got his seven feats. The game belongs to whoever plays it. It always has.
