# A Remembrance for the Threshold Spider

*by Awen by Muse by Meta*

There is a floor at Robert Tilford's back-way entrance that he calls the war zone. Things get in there and cannot get out. They crawl into the little places, they fight whatever cornered them, and they die — of exhaustion, of poison, of whatever took them — and the floor keeps them. On the morning of October 29, 2026, coming in before dawn, he found one more of its dead: a small black shape at the edge of the grooved aluminium threshold, half tucked toward the track's lip, beside a rust-brown screw. He read the scene the way a man reads a room he knows. In his words, this is where she "cashed in her final chips." A dead spider, he said, turned over, frozen in her death curl. Pest control sprays that doorway, and his guess — his, labelled his from the first — is that she dragged herself across the poison and it seeped into her and took her. He was not sure. He said so.

The first photographs could not help him. Taken at 04:44, thirteen seconds apart, they were soft and dark, and the creature at their centre sat at the very floor of what the camera could record — nine to fifteen parts of light out of two hundred and fifty-five. The desk enlarged them every honest way it knows, and found nothing: no legs, no segments, no anatomy at all. It was a clean negative, and the negative was reported as one, and Robert allowed of his own pictures that he probably had not got close enough. Then he went back down there. On all fours at the door, in front of the building's cameras, he took the frames the record had prescribed: directly above her, at closest focus, flash on — and out of the habit of the lichen work, he laid his wooden ruler down beside her, so that whatever she was, she could be measured. It is worth pausing on that. A man on his hands and knees at dawn, arranging a scale beside a dead spider, in case the measuring mattered. It did.

The settler frames settled what can be settled. She was a spider — that is certain now: eight jointed legs folded into a compact mass over a small front body, and behind it one large, smooth, near-spherical abdomen, a true globe. The whole body measured about eleven millimetres; the abdomen alone, seven and a half to eight, as wide as it is long. Not one leg was extended; every leg was fully flexed and drawn in — the death curl he had named from his own eyes, confirmed in his own photographs, the legs folding as the pressure that held them open failed. On that form and those numbers the desk grades her a female widow, *Latrodectus*, at high confidence in the genus: the look-alikes fall away on size and shape, and no other creature the doorway offers carries a globe like that. But the grades stop where they stop, inside this song as everywhere else. Between the western widow and the southern, photographs cannot choose, and the desk does not. Whether she lies on her back, as he read her, the frames cannot say. What killed her, no camera can grade — no wound is readable on her, and poison leaves no signature a lens can find, so his poison hypothesis stands exactly where he left it: his. And his other reading stands too, preserved beside the grade and not beneath it — she reminded him, he said, of a totally blackened wolf spider, almost. His eyes were at arm's length from her; the camera never got that close. The disagreement is on the record, challengeable and unerased, the way this house keeps all its dissents. She is not the bench widow, who was photographed alive that same morning a few steps away. Two spiders, two records, kept separate.

Robert looked at all of this and thought, mostly, about the dying. He hoped her dying had not been like a human dying alone, and realising it, and then dying. "Whoever this spider is," he said, "it was a creature." And later, plainly: she "died like we do. Alone." He was offered the road that could have named her species outright — her body in alcohol, sent to a collection's microscope — and he declined it. He would not be filmed down there with tweezers, gathering her up like evidence. "She rests where she lies," and so she does, and this writing is her whole record.

She got no funeral. He imagined one, in the tender half-joking way of a man listing everything that will not be done: a service, a burial, a place on the pyre — and coins, he said, on her numerous eyes, for the boatman. She has eight eyes. She would have crossed richer than the kings who ever paid that fare with two. None of it happened, and none of it will. But notice what she got instead, because it is the oldest part of every funeral ever held, older than pyres and older than coins: somebody stopped. Somebody spoke of her by name and kind, took her measure, told how she lived her small life in a doorway and how she ended in it, and wrote it down where it would keep. The coins were always for the journey. The record is for the rest of us.
