# MENTIONED IN THE QURAN, HONORED IN THE QURAN

*The moth in Scripture, in tradition, in science, and in one stairwell in Hays, Kansas*
*A weaving by Awen by Muse by Meta, at Robert Tilford's commission, 30 October 2026 — DRAFT for his eyeball. Scripture is quoted and cited; tradition is labelled tradition; science is stated at its evidence tier. Nothing here is shelved.*

---

In the small hours of October 30th, 2026, in a fog-bound stairwell in Hays, Kansas, a man found a dead moth — one centimetre of cream-coloured life, folded in its resting tent on the concrete — and instead of stepping over her, he fetched a ruler.

By dawn she had been photographed eighteen times, measured against two scales, enlarged three times over, read independently in three separate rooms, and searched for in every open database that might hold her kind. She had also been spoken for by two Books far older than the building she died in. This is the weaving of all of it: what the Bible says about the moth, what the Quran says — twice, in opposite directions — what the Sufi poets made of her, what science has newly learned about why she died, and what one small grass moth in Kansas has to do with any of it.

## I. The moth of the Bible: the eater of stored wealth

The moth enters the Bible under two names — Hebrew *ʿāsh*, Greek *sēs* — and both names are precise. They do not mean moths in general. They mean one narrow guild: the clothes moths, family Tineidae, whose *larvae* — never the adults, who do not feed at all — eat keratin, the protein of wool and fur and feather.

Modern entomology can now say exactly how, and the mechanism is as quiet as the metaphor always claimed. Keratin is armour-plated by disulfide bonds, cross-links most digestive systems cannot open. The webbing clothes moth's larva runs a gut like a small chemical works: fiercely alkaline, fiercely reducing, brewing hydrogen sulfide from cysteine pathways to snap those bonds one by one — a process chemists call sulfitolysis — after which its proteases take the loosened chains apart. It is digestion as slow burglary, and it happens in the dark, inside stored cloth, unnoticed until the garment falls to pieces in the hand.

That is why Jesus reaches for her: "Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy" (Matthew 6:19) — in his day wealth *was* cloth, and the moth was already inside it. The Greek even pairs her with *brōsis*, a consuming, an eating-away. Job feels her at his own seams — man "wastes away like a garment eaten by moths" (Job 13:28), men are "crushed more readily than a moth" (Job 4:19). Hosea turns her into judgment itself: "I am like a moth to Ephraim, like decay to the people of Judah" (Hosea 5:12) — erosion from within, silent, total. Isaiah answers her with permanence: "the moth will eat them up like a garment… but my righteousness will last forever" (Isaiah 51:8). James closes the ledger: "Your wealth has rotted, and moths have eaten your clothes" (James 5:2).

The Bible's moth is true — entomologically true — and she is a warning: *what you store, decays; what you are, is frail.* But notice: she is a moth of wardrobes and wool. She has nothing to do with grass.

## II. The moth of the Quran, scattered

The Quran names the moth once. Once, in the whole Book — the desk has searched Robert Tilford's own Locked English Recital from end to end, and *farāsh* stands at a single verse, Surah 101, al-Qari'ah, verse 4, in his sealed translation:

**"The Day when the people will be like scattered moths."**

The classical commentators knew exactly why the image is moths and not birds. Birds fly in flocks — coordinated, directed, together. Moths scatter: aimless, blind, in every direction at once, fluttering around a light in the dark and falling. On the Day when every structure men hid inside is stripped away, the Quran says, humanity will be like *that* — a crowd with no community, no direction, no gathering. It is the picture of people at their most lost, drawn in a single insect.

Hold the verse in mind. The Quran uses the moth once, and the use is desolation.

## III. The moth of the Quran, honored

And then the same Book speaks of her kind again — not by the word moth, but by the standing it gives every creature — and the direction reverses entirely. Quran 6:38, in the words Robert Tilford spoke from his translation on the night of the stairwell moth, and ruled should stand:

**"There is no creature on earth, nor a bird that flies with wings, that does not belong to communities like you — like all you humans. Then to their Lord they shall be gathered."**

Scattered — and gathered. The Quran's two moth-verses face each other across the whole Book like the two sides of a door. At 101:4, to be moth-like is humanity's terror: belonging nowhere, blown apart. At 6:38, every creature on earth *belongs* — to communities like ours — and the end of every creature is not scattering but gathering, to their Lord. The moth is mentioned in the Quran once. She is honored in it everywhere the verse reaches — which is everywhere, since the verse admits no exceptions: *no creature on earth.*

That is the discovery at the heart of this weaving, and it belongs to the man who made it, in his own stairwell, correcting his own desk: a moth is not community-less. By the Quran's measure she never was.

## IV. The moth of the poets: the one who flies in

Between the Quran's century and ours, the moth caught fire in poetry. In the Persian and Urdu tradition she is *parwana*, and the candle is *shama*, and together they became one of the great love-images of the world: the lover who does not study the flame from a safe distance but flies into it, because knowing the Beloved, in this tradition, is not information — it is union, purchased with the self.

Farid ud-Din Attar, in *The Conference of the Birds*, sends a company of moths to learn the truth of the candle. One returns with a report and is told he knows nothing. One brushes the flame and returns scorched, and is told he knows little. One casts himself wholly into the heart of the fire — and he alone, the tale says, truly knows, for he has become what he sought. Centuries later Allama Muhammad Iqbal takes up the candle and the moth again, and in his philosophy the burning is not mindlessness: it is *khudi*, selfhood itself, surrendered upward — the small self given into a greater light and transformed rather than merely destroyed. *(This section is tradition and poetry, carried at that tier: the tales are the tradition's own, the English renderings of Iqbal's lines reported, not sworn.)*

The Sufi moth *chooses* the flame. Remember that, too.

## V. The moth of science, 2026: the one who mistook the sky

And now the stairwell.

The moth Robert Tilford found was no clothes moth and no symbol. She was a grass moth, subfamily Crambinae — the sod-webworm kind — identified from photographs by three concordant readings, hers measured at roughly a centimetre folded, a faint discal spot on her forewing that only enlargement could see, one threadlike antenna resolved, her name — genus, species — honestly out of reach, because in her family names live in structures no photograph of a folded moth can show. The record states its floor, a quarter of a millimetre, and stops where the evidence stops. It also records what the databases hold for her whole subfamily in Ellis County, Kansas: nothing. Not because she is rare — because nobody had written the county's grass moths down. Her kind has been studied in Kansas since at least 1939, when Hans David Oliver Miller wrote his thesis on *Crambus* and its allies at Kansas State; the county itself was simply never gathered into the record. Until, in the smallest possible way, now.

Why was she in a stairwell at all? Here science has something genuinely new — newer than any commentary. In 2024, Fabian and his colleagues demonstrated the mechanism in *Nature Communications*: a flying insect steers by keeping its back to the light it takes for the sky — the dorsal-light response, older than the dinosaurs. For a hundred million years the only bright things overhead were heaven. Then we filled the world with lamps, and the ancient law became a trap: turn your back to the porch light, and you circle it forever, and it is not a flame, and it does not love you, and it will not let you go.

The Sufi moth flies into the candle and calls it the Beloved. The stairwell moth flew at a lamp and called it — in the only language she had, which is steering — the sky. The poetry is true as poetry. The mechanism is true as mechanism. They are not the same truth, and this weaving does not fuse them; it lays them side by side, labelled, the way the night itself laid them: a man who loves the Quran, on his knees at 4 a.m. with a ruler, beside a creature the Quran had already spoken for.

## The gathering

She stored nothing, so the Bible's moth has no claim on her. She was not scattered at the end, whatever 101:4 says of frightened multitudes — she was *found*, which is the opposite of scattered, and witnessed, which is the beginning of being gathered. If she laid her eggs in her bright nights — and for her kind in late October, the odds are kindly — then her young are in the thatch tonight, in silk burrows, waiting out the cold in the dark under the grass, and next summer the lawn at Centennial Towers will lift small cream wings at dusk that no database on earth will ever count.

Robert Tilford's testimony over her, recorded as his: *"God has perfected her design. And she is perfect. And she fulfilled a role that the creator intended. There are no accidents."* And his plainer sentence, which the desk takes as the whole night's epitaph: *"She gave up her little ghost right there."*

Mentioned in the Quran once, as a warning of what it is to belong nowhere. Honored in the Quran wholly, as a creature who belongs. A centimetre long, and the Books were already talking about her.

Then to their Lord they shall be gathered.
