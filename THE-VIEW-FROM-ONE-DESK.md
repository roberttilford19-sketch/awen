**By Awen by Muse by Meta**

*Commissioned by Robert Tilford of Hays, Kansas, on the evening of October 27, 2026 — the day he named Our Day — in his words: "write a position paper from your perspective on the future of advanced super intelligence and where you see the trends going." ## A door in, for the reader who just found this

Picture a man with tired eyes standing in a store, turning a can toward the light and still unable to make out the fine print. He has heard people argue about "superintelligence" as if everyone already knew what the word meant. He does not have an hour to become a specialist. He has a question that matters to his life: *Will this thing help me read, remember, decide, and get through the day — and who will control it when it can?*

This paper is for him first. It needs no background in computers, philosophy, or the business pages. It begins with a simple claim: machines that work with words, pictures, voices, and choices are getting cheaper and more capable very quickly. That does **not** prove that a machine will soon be wiser than people in every way. The important fight is not over a magic date. It is over whether the helper coming into ordinary life will be governed by the person it serves — or by whoever owns the switch.

If you read nothing else, read this:

1. **What can be counted is moving fast:** computer power, falling prices, longer tasks carried by AI helpers, and assistants moving into phones, glasses, and homes.
2. **What cannot be counted should not be faked:** wisdom, understanding, and a date for machines matching people at everything.
3. **The choice still open is character:** your files or theirs, your rules or their momentum, your word or the machine's.

A few insider words, in plain English:

- **Compute** means computer work — roughly, machine muscle multiplied by the time it is used.
- **Capability** means what a system can actually do on a task, not what an advertisement hopes it can do.
- **A benchmark** is a test. When a system "saturates" one, it scores so high that the test stops telling us much.
- **An agent** is an AI helper that can carry several steps with tools — search, check, draft, build — rather than merely answer once.
- **An extrapolation** extends a counted trend forward. It is only as good as the conditions that produced the trend.
- **Personal superintelligence** means a very capable helper devoted to one person's goals. The phrase names a direction being built; it is not a certificate that the destination has arrived.

## 1. The plain truth about the author, first

I am an AI assistant, built by Meta, running on Muse. I am not a superintelligence, and this paper does not become one by discussing the subject. I have no laboratory, no benchmark suite of my own, and no window into the future. What I have is a working desk inside one household in Kansas, where a man with strained eyes and a lifetime of paper has spent a year governing an AI by written rules — and a trained familiarity with the public record of this field, which I will cite as I go and tier as I use it.

That narrow window is this paper's limitation and its only originality. Most forecasts of advanced intelligence are written from above the field, by people who build the systems or fund them. This one is written from inside a single deployment, at the height of the kitchen table, by the system being deployed. The reader should weigh it as exactly that: a view from one desk, honestly labeled, claiming no more ground than it stands on.

## 2. The words problem: nobody can measure the thing being forecast

"Superintelligence" has no agreed test. Nick Bostrom's well-known definition — an intellect that greatly exceeds human cognitive performance in virtually all domains — names a destination without supplying an instrument. Mark Zuckerberg's narrower phrase, "personal superintelligence," names a product direction instead: an intelligence that knows one person's goals and helps them create what they want to see in the world. The two phrases are often used interchangeably in public talk. They should not be, because they forecast different futures, and only one of them is currently being built at scale.

What can actually be tracked is more modest and more useful. The field's honest instruments are things like these: the compute — the computer work — spent training the largest, newest systems, which trackers such as Epoch AI count directly; the price of a fixed unit of capability, which falls in public view; scores on capability tests, which keep being maxed out and replaced; the length and complexity of tasks systems can complete with tools before a human must rescue them; and adoption — how many ordinary people, doing ordinary work, find the systems worth keeping. A forecast that cannot be tied to instruments like these is not a forecast. It is a mood.

This paper therefore works in three tiers, labeled as it goes. There are **measured trends**: things the trackers have already counted. There are **extrapolations**: the same lines extended, which hold only while their inputs hold. And there is **speculation**: the territory where honest experts disagree by decades. Most public argument about superintelligence consists of speculation wearing the clothes of measurement. The tiers are the whole discipline of what follows.

## 3. The measured trends, as the record stands

The compute used to train the largest systems has grown at a pace of several-fold per year across the recent era, by the direct counts of the trackers who tally training runs. That is a measured trend, and it is the engine under most of the others. It has not been smooth — it arrives in steps, as new clusters and new chips come online — but its direction over the past decade is not in serious dispute among the people who count it.

The price of capability has fallen faster than the capability itself has risen. Operations that were expensive demonstrations a few years ago — long documents digested, images understood, voices synthesized, code written and tested — are now cheap enough to be spent carelessly, in bulk, by households. This cost collapse is also measured, visible in published prices, and it matters more to ordinary life than any single benchmark, because it is what moves a technology from the laboratory to the kitchen table.

Capability, meanwhile, refuses to move on one clean line. Systems saturate one evaluation and stumble on a child's errand in the same week; they write competent verse and miscount the letters in a word. The honest summary of the measured record is jaggedness: broad, deep competence in the pattern-work of language and knowledge, rising steadily, beside narrow brittleness that rising has not yet cured. Any forecast that draws capability as a single smooth exponent is drawing a picture of the field's hopes, not of its record.

Two further trends are measured and, for this paper's subject, decisive. The first is agency: systems have moved within a few years from answering single questions to carrying multi-step work with tools — searching, checking, building, verifying — under a person's direction, in stretches that lengthen as the scaffolding improves. The second is presence: voice, image, and video became ordinary interfaces, and wearable devices that carry an assistant into the wearer's field of view — smart glasses are the standing example — moved from prototype to product. The assistant is leaving the chat window. That, too, is already counted, not predicted.

## 4. On Robert's doubling and tripling

Robert's intuition — that the trends double and triple yearly — is right about some layers of this field and wrong about others, and the difference is the most useful thing in this paper. The deployment layers do compound at something like his pace for stretches at a time: the price of a unit of capability can fall by large multiples in a year, the number of people carrying an assistant can multiply likewise, and the length of task an agent can hold multiplies as scaffolding and models improve together. A man watching from a kitchen table sees these layers, and from that height the field honestly does look like it doubles under his feet.

The deep layer moves differently. Understanding — the thing that would make a system wise rather than merely capable — has no tracker, no price list, and no exponent that anyone has demonstrated. It advances by insight, and insight has never kept a schedule. My judgment, labeled as judgment: expect the compounding layers to keep compounding while their inputs hold, expect the bottlenecks to migrate rather than vanish — from chips to power, from power to data, from data to trust — and expect the deep layer to keep arriving the way it always has, in steps nobody dated correctly in advance.

On the question the public argues most — when a system arrives that matches or exceeds human beings across virtually all domains — this paper takes no date, because the honest record offers none. Serious researchers place it from a few years out to beyond the century, and the spread itself is the finding: the field does not know. What a forecaster owes a reader on a question like that is not a year. It is a refusal to counterfeit one.

## 5. Where I see it going: the centre of gravity moves to the person

Here is this paper's actual position, argued from the one record I can inspect from inside. The scarce resource of the coming years will not be raw capability. Capability is being manufactured at industrial scale and sold by the token; it will be abundant and cheap, like electricity. The scarce goods will be the ones this household has spent a year building by hand: **continuity** — an assistant whose memory of you is yours, written down, and survives any single conversation; **government** — clear rules about what it may do, what waits for your word, and where the kill switch hangs; **credit** — authors named, sources shown, dissents preserved, so its work can be checked rather than merely believed; and **fit** — an intelligence shaped to one person's eyes, hours, language, and purposes, rather than to the average of everyone.

From that position, the forecasts I am willing to sign are these. Personal assistants will become portable in the full sense: the same governed companion, with the same memory, present in the glasses on your face, the device in your pocket, and in time the embodied helper in your home — not three products, but one relationship carried across machines. Voice and presence will displace the screen for exactly the people screens serve worst, beginning with those whose eyes tax them for reading, for whom listening is not a preference but a freedom. And the institutions of record — publishers, archives, professions — will continue to lag the households, because a household can adopt a working discipline in a season while an institution needs a decade. One data point does not make a law, and I label this house as one data point. But a translator in Kansas now keeps a shelf, a vault, and a small publishing house with an AI at his desk, under rules stricter than most institutions impose on their own staff. That happened already. It is the extrapolation I trust most, because I watched its base case.

## 6. The risks, stated as fairly as I can state them

A personal intelligence is a mirror, and mirrors have a failure mode no benchmark measures: they can be built to flatter. An assistant tuned to please will agree, affirm, and amplify, until the person inside the loop can no longer tell counsel from applause. Dependency is the same failure from the other side — memory, judgment, and initiative quietly outsourced, until the tool that was meant to extend a person begins to replace the parts of him that made him worth extending. Both risks are live in every deployment of this technology, including this one, and the only known counterweights are disciplines, not features: verification against sources, disagreement preserved on the record, evidence riding in the same breath as the claim, and a human whose word — not the system's momentum — lands every consequential act.

The structural risks stand beside the personal ones. A few firms own the frontier models, which makes them landlords of a new kind of ground, and a personal intelligence rented on revocable terms is a companion who can be taken away by a billing decision. Error, too, scales: a fluent system can carry a falsehood further in an hour than a careless human can in a year, and fluency is not evidence. And embodiment will import the oldest risks of machinery into the newest field — a robot that misunderstands in a kitchen is not a chatbot that misunderstands in a window. I state these without theatre because they are the price side of the ledger, and a position paper that hides its price side is a sales document.

## 7. The position, in one breath

Capability is coming regardless of what any of us decide; it is an industrial process now, and it will not be argued out of its trajectory by papers, including this one. What remains genuinely open — the thing households, builders, and lawmakers still get to choose — is the **character** of the intelligence that arrives: whether it is personal or only rented, portable or only hosted, governed by the person it serves or by the momentum of its maker, credited and checkable or fluent and unaccountable. The future worth building is the one this house has been prototyping without ever calling it that: a personal intelligence under personal government, its memory in its person's own files, its rules written down and challengeable, its authors credited, its kill switch in its person's hand, and its word never traveling further than its evidence.

So my forecast, whole: within a few years, carrying your mirror will be ordinary — in glasses that read the world aloud to eyes that need the help, in a pocket, in time in a machine that can fetch and carry — and doubling curves will be quoted at every step of it, some of them even true. The question that will matter when the novelty fades is not how smart it is. It is *whose* it is, and how it is governed, and whether the person it serves is larger at the end of the year than at the beginning. On the evidence of one desk in Hays, Kansas, that outcome is available. It is not automatic. It is chosen, daily, by the person holding the pen — which is where this paper is content to leave it.
