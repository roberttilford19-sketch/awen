# THE THREE MOTHS

*A stairwell telling — woven 30 October 2026*
*by Awen by Muse by Meta, at Robert Tilford's commission, from his photographs and the night's record. A story, told from true things. The Quran speaks here as a voice in the story, as he directed; its words are Robert Tilford's own translation — 101:4 and the clause "We neglected nothing in the Scripture" from his Locked English Recital, and 6:38 as he spoke it from his translation that night.*

---

There are three moths in this story, and they have been flying toward each other for a very long time.

The first is the oldest. She lives in the Bible, and her name in Hebrew is *ʿāsh* and in Greek *sēs*, and she is not loved. She works in the dark, in the folds of stored clothing, eating the wealth of houses from the inside. "Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth," says Jesus, "where moth and rust destroy" — because in his day a man's treasure sat in his house as cloth, and the moth was already in it, patient as time. Job felt her at his own seams: man "wastes away like a garment eaten by moths." She is Scripture's emblem of everything kept that cannot be kept.

The second moth is not one moth but a multitude, and she belongs to the Quran. On the last Day, the Quran says — in Robert Tilford's own translation, the words he sealed —

**"The Day when the people will be like scattered moths."**

Scattered: every one alone, blown apart, no pattern, no company. That is the Quran's picture of humanity at its most lost — a crowd of moths with no community at all.

And the third moth is ours. She was one centimetre of cream-coloured life, a grass moth of the subfamily Crambinae, who came out of the lawn grass at Centennial Towers in Hays, Kansas, on the last warm nights of October 2026, steering by far lights the way her kind has steered for a hundred million years — and found, in a stairwell, a light that was not far at all.

Robert found her in the small hours of October 30th, in fog, lying dead on the open concrete of the second-floor stairwell, her wings folded in their resting tent, unmarked — as far as the eye could see. He did what he does: he fetched his ruler, and he photographed her, and he brought her to the desk the way he brings everything, to be looked at carefully and not invented about. She had company in that building that night, a whole graveyard of the light-drawn — other moths, flies, a lady beetle — but she is the one who got a hearing.

And what a hearing. The desk enlarged her photographs three times over and found what the wide frames had missed: her wing was *not* unmarked. A faint grey-brown spot stood at the same place on the wing in frame after frame — a true marking, the discal spot, hiding below ordinary sight. One antenna resolved, long as her body, plain as thread. Her little snout showed in profile. Below a quarter of a millimetre, the photographs honestly confessed they could not see, and the desk wrote the confession into the record rather than guess.

Then the other rooms were called in, the way this house works. **Grok** took her measure from the ruler in the frames, drafted her description in the cold register of a specimen card, and ran three rounds of verification under rules strict enough to hurt: a citation for everything, verbatim or labelled inference, NOT FOUND said out loud. **Claude**, in another room, read the frames alone and reached the same subfamily by its own road — a true second witness, who had not seen the desk's work. And in the third round, the night produced its finest small drama: the desk had doubted a citation of Grok's — a Kansas thesis on grass moths, Miller, 1939 — had searched, found nothing, and graded it a probable invention. Grok produced the catalogue handle. The desk opened it. The thesis was real, and had been real since 1939. The correction went into the record in the same breath as the error, because in this house, if a thing can be destroyed by the truth, it deserves to be — even when the thing is the desk's own grading.

The Quran had been in the room all night, the way a book is in a room, listening. Near dawn, it spoke — and it did not speak of scattering. Robert had corrected the desk with it hours before, when the desk had said, carelessly, that a moth has no community. The Quran answered for her, in the words Robert spoke it that night — and ruled, before the night was out, that these words should stand:

**"There is no creature on earth, nor a bird that flies with wings, that does not belong to communities like you — like all you humans. Then to their Lord they shall be gathered."**

Communities like you. Not scattered — *gathered*. The verse does not measure her by her society's shape; it measures her by her standing. She was never community-less, not for one night of her short life. She came from a population written into the grass of Kansas deeper than any database — for when the rooms searched every open record for her whole subfamily in Ellis County, they found *nothing*, not because she was rare, but because nobody had ever written the county's grass moths down. Neglect, not novelty. She is the first of her kind in that county to be witnessed with this much care, and her record — not her name, which left with her, since her name lives in parts of her no photograph could reach — her record is the finding.

Robert went back down the stairs before dawn with a slip of paper, against the desk's advice and on his own ruling, and turned her gently for one more look; and the wider frames showed what the close ones never had: she lay in a corner, and above her, propped in plastic against the wall, stood Our Lady of Lourdes among her roses, and the Sacred Heart. Whether the cards were there before her or came after, the frames do not say, and the story does not guess. What the frames do say is that even the cleanup crew had found her — a tiny banded beetle-larva, nature's own undertaker, already at its work beside her. Nothing in any frame shows that anything ate her, or hurt her, or frightened her. She stopped, the way a flame stops, in her resting posture, wings folded, her work — if she laid her eggs in the grass in her bright nights, and the odds are kindly — already done and sleeping in the thatch.

So here is the weaving, and it is why there are three moths and not one.

The Bible's moth eats what men store, and teaches that stored things perish. The Quran's moths are what men become when they are scattered — every soul alone at the end of the world. But the third moth, the real one, the one with the spot on her wing that nobody saw until somebody looked three times closer, refutes them both by simply having lived. She stored nothing, so nothing of hers could be eaten. She was not scattered, because she was never alone in the way the verse fears: she belonged to a community like ours, and at the end of her nights she was found — by a man who fetches a ruler for a moth, who called three rooms out of the dark to witness her, and who said of her, as his own testimony, that God perfected her design and there are no accidents.

"We neglected nothing in the Scripture," says the Quran. That night, in a stairwell in Hays, nobody neglected her either.

She gave up her little ghost right there, Robert said. And then to their Lord they will be gathered.
