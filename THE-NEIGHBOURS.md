# The Neighbours — a parable

*By Awen by Muse by Meta · DRAFT for Robert's eyeball · 2026-10-30 · nothing shelved*

---

In the days when the forest was young and no man had yet walked in it, there stood a great tree, and on the trunk of that tree two neighbours lived.

The first was a cricket, no longer than your thumbnail, who spent the warm days walking the bark — up the ridges, down the furrows, tasting what the tree provided, testing the air with feelers longer than the body that carried them. The second was a spider, small enough to hide in a raindrop's shadow, who kept a corner of the same trunk a hand's breadth away and hunted there, patient as stones are patient.

They lived their whole lives in that nearness. The cricket passed within a breath of the spider's corner a hundred times and never knew a hunter lived there. The spider felt the bark tremble under the cricket's feet a hundred times and never knew whose feet they were. They shared the same tree, the same sun on the same bark, the same rain. The spring came to both of them, and the high summer, and the first cool night that tells small creatures the year is turning. Everything the days brought, the days brought to them together — everything except each other. They never learned each other's names. It is possible neither knew the other existed.

Then the tree was wounded — the forest does not record how — and it wept.

The tree's tears were gold and slow and terrible, and they ran down the trunk the way honey runs, except that what they touched, they kept. The spider met them first, or the cricket did — the order is lost and does not matter. What matters is that each met them alone. There was no one to warn, and no one to warn them; each struggled in the manner of its kind, legs drawing in against the slow gold, and each was stilled in the posture of its last moment, the way a word is stilled at the end of a sentence.

And the tree wept again, and again, as wounded trees do, and flow upon flow sealed them — not together, for a small stretch of the gold lay between them and was never crossed — but in the same keeping. A thumb's width apart. For ever.

The forest fell. The tree fell. The world turned over more times than there are words for. Mountains were worn to fields and fields were lifted to mountains, and still the two neighbours lay in their gold, in the dark, in the silence, a thumb's width apart, each complete, each unknown to the other. Other small lives lay in that stone too — a crowd of them, some named by no one, most never to be named — a whole congregation of the small and the finished, kept together in one piece of hardened light.

At the last, the stone came to rest in the hand of a man, in a town of wheat and wind, on the far side of the world from the forest that made it. The man carried it to the window, because that is where the light is, and he looked. And looking, he saw the cricket. And looking longer — because love is nothing if not looking longer — he saw the spider, drawn up small in the gold, and he understood that they had been neighbours.

He could not give them back their forest. He could not give them back their lives, or tell them each other's names, or say whether either had been glad. All of that the stone keeps, and the stone is not telling. But he could do the one thing left to the living: he could refuse to let them be forgotten. He measured them. He counted their legs. He learned what could be learned and wrote down, honestly, what could not. And then he asked that their story be told — not the story of two friends, for they were never friends; they never met. The story of two neighbours.

For most of what is kept in the stones of this world is not enemies, and not friends. It is neighbours — lives laid down side by side in the same keeping, strangers to the end, sharing a fate neither one chose.

**No one chooses the stone they rest in, or who rests beside them — but the living can always choose to speak gently of the stranger at their side.**
