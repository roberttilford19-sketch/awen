# The Spider Who Did Not Run

## A research note on black widow behaviour, prepared at Robert Tilford's request

*By Awen by Muse by Meta — October 29, 2026*

The scene is Robert's, from his own photographs. At dawn on October 29, 2026, beneath a bench at Centennial Towers in Hays, Kansas, an adult female widow — *Latrodectus*, almost certainly the western black widow, *Latrodectus hesperus* — was sitting out of her web, on the flat exposed frame of the bench beside a small rusty bolt. When his hand and phone came up under the bench with the flash firing, she did not run. Across two frames eight seconds apart, her legs shifted into a braced, raised posture, turned toward him. Robert's reading of that posture is bravery. The desk graded it as defensive behaviour and offered three possible explanations — that she was cornered with no retreat, that she may have been guarding an egg sac, and that she may simply have frozen at the startle. Robert added a fourth of his own: territory — she may have been holding her corner. He also asked a plain seasonal question: isn't it too late in the year, with the cold coming on, for babies?

This note reports what the extension record and the research literature actually support, claim by claim, with the sources named beside the claims. Nothing in it amends the widow essay or any other record; it is a research note, and it ends where the evidence ends.

## 1. The defensive sequence: what a widow does when something comes at her

The best experimental answer comes from Nelsen and colleagues, whose 2014 study in *Animal Behaviour* ("Poke but don't pinch: risk assessment and venom metering in the western black widow spider, *Latrodectus hesperus*") attacked widows with gelatin fingers at three threat levels and catalogued what the spiders did. The widow's defensive repertoire, as they define it, has five entries: **move** (the whole body, usually away), **retract the legs** (draw them in toward the body), **silk-flick** (the hind legs draw viscous glue-silk from the spinnerets and extend toward the threat to throw it on), **play dead** (drop from the web, all legs retracted against the body, motionless), and **bite**. The escalation matters more than the list. Against a single poke, no spider bit; most simply moved away. Against persistent prodding, roughly half flicked silk, some played dead, and only one spider in forty-three attempted a bite. Biting became common — sixty percent — only when the spider was pinched and held between two fingers, which is to say, grasped by a predator with escape already impossible. The widow, in short, is a risk assessor, not a fighter: she spends the cheap defences first and keeps the bite for the moment there is nothing else left.

The older literature fills in the two ends of that sequence. At the fleeing end, genus accounts describe a threatened widow dropping from her web to the ground on a silk safety line, and retreat into the web's own funnel is the standard first answer to disturbance. The death-feint is documented across the genus: Vetter's 2008 account of the brown widow, *Latrodectus geometricus*, describes thanatosis — leaping from the web, retracting the legs, and playing dead — when the protective retreat is disturbed, and the behaviour can be prolonged. At the standing end stands the widow's signature defence, described in a 1982 study in *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*: a threatened mature female draws out a strand of adhesive, viscous silk with her fourth pair of legs and *spreads her appendages* to drape that silk over her own delicate abdomen — and to place it onto the offender if need be. In laboratory trials the viscous silk measurably protected widows from predatory mice, and females whose spinnerets were experimentally blocked escaped less often. Genus reviews summarising Vetter's work put the bite's condition in one clause: when a widow is trapped she prefers to play dead or flick silk, and bites occur *when she cannot escape*.

Two facts about her senses and her address complete the picture. Widows have very poor eyesight and know the world almost entirely through vibration carried by the web — the University of California Statewide IPM Program's spider guide and the genus accounts agree on this — so a widow out of her web is a spider whose early-warning system has been unplugged. And she is almost never out of it voluntarily: Colorado State University Extension's fact sheet on the western widow (Fact Sheet 5.605) states that adult females "usually remain in their webs unless forced out by adverse temperatures or destruction of their web," that they do not forage away from it, and that widow spiders "rarely, if ever, bite when not within a web." Her whole defensive architecture assumes the web: a retreat to vanish into, a safety line to drop on, silk underfoot to read the threat through. On a flat, open bench frame at close quarters, every one of those is gone, and what remains is the close-range end of the repertoire — freeze, silk, or stand.

One question in Robert's scene the literature does *not* answer by name: the braced posture with the front legs raised. None was found after research — no formally named "front legs raised" threat display for *Latrodectus* appears in the sources searched, and Nelsen and colleagues' standard ethogram for the western widow contains no such entry; its leg behaviours are retraction (legs pulled in) and the silk-flick (hind legs extended toward the threat). The raised, braced set in Robert's frames is therefore described in this note, not named: it sits closest to a defensive alert set, and to the appendage-spreading that opens the 1982 silk defence, but the literature searched does not catalogue it as a display of its own.

## 2. Egg-sac guarding: does a mother widow defend her sac?

Yes — at the strength of attendance, and with active defence demonstrated against the enemies that actually threaten a sac. The University of Michigan's Animal Diversity Web account of the southern black widow states the basic fact plainly: the egg sac is "camouflaged, guarded, or carried by the female," and "the female rarely leaves the web; she watches over the eggs in the egg sac until the spiderlings hatch." The Encyclopedia of Life's northern widow account likewise notes that the egg sac "can be seen within the irregular web, guarded by the female."

Extension grades that attendance as defence. The Colorado State University fact sheet says it in so many words: "Bites may be more likely if the female is tending an egg sac in the web, **which she will defend**." A clinical overview of *Latrodectus* in the medical literature makes the same point from the other side: "With the exception of females protecting a… whitish egg sac, these spiders are usually not aggressive." And the active component is no longer anecdote. In a 2025 study in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, Arabesky and colleagues exposed two widow species, *Latrodectus geometricus* and *Latrodectus pallidus*, to an egg-sac parasitoid wasp: females of both species responded with guarding behaviour — increased activity, tapping and circling the sac, body shaking — and *geometricus* females went further, evading and killing the wasp. Field work by Moura and colleagues, cited in the same study, found that an attending female's presence reduced parasitism of her sac roughly threefold.

The honest boundaries of that finding are two. The demonstrated defence is aimed at spider-sized enemies — parasitoid wasps are the sac's real predators — and no source found shows a sac-guarding widow escalating against a human-sized disturber into anything beyond the standard defensive sequence; guarding raises her defensiveness, it does not change its nature. And guarding is done *at the sac*, in the web. A female out on an open frame, away from the corner where any sac would hang, is not in the guarding posture the literature describes, whatever she may have been walking to or from.

## 3. Seasonality: Robert's question, answered head-on for Kansas

Robert's instinct is sound: it *is* late in the season, and the calendar is the strongest single witness in this whole inquiry. Widows mate in late spring and early summer — the University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum's black widow account gives mating "most often in late spring and early summer," with females producing several egg masses during the summer; Kansas State's *Common Kansas Spiders* pocket guide says of the northern widow that mating occurs in May and the female "produces several egg sacs during the summer," each holding roughly 118 to 395 eggs. The usual seasonal count across extension sources is four to nine sacs in one summer (the University of Hawaii's extension knowledge base allows ten to twenty over a whole lifetime). A Kansas pest note for Medicine Lodge puts the local peak in August: females fully grown, and "probably guarding an egg sac that will soon hatch."

Now run the clock forward from a laying. Eggs incubate about twenty to thirty days (UC Statewide IPM). The spiderlings hatch inside the sac, remain in it for several more days and moult there, then cluster near the mother only until the yolk they hatched with is used up, and then disperse, most of them ballooning away on silk (Colorado State University). The whole cycle — laying to an empty sac — is a month to six weeks in warm weather, and it stretches as temperatures fall, because, as Colorado State puts it, in cold weather "spider development outdoors ceases." So: the sacs of high summer were hatched and scattered by September. A sac laid in late August or September could still be in play into October, especially with cold nights slowing it — a late, live, still-guarded sac on October 29 is *possible*. But it asks for a late laying and a slow clock together, and the season does not favour requiring both.

The alternative needs no special pleading at all. Colorado State again: "Black widows survive winter as either mature or immature forms. During cold weather… the spiders seek refuge under stones or other cover," and many move into shelter by autumn. Females are long-lived for spiders — commonly more than a year, and as long as three years on Kaston's 1970 record, as the Arkansas museum account reports it. A plump, active adult female, sitting at dawn on October 29 in the sheltered underside corner of a bench, reads without strain as exactly that: a mature female holding a protected microhabitat into the cold — the corner being precisely the kind of stone-and-cover refuge the extension record describes her kind seeking for the winter. Her plumpness fits a well-fed female at the season's end; the record cannot grade it further than that. **The seasonality verdict: the season favours the corner-holder over the still-guarding mother — while leaving the mother possible, at the lower likelihood a late sac would give her.**

## 4. The honest limit

Behavioural science can say what a widow in this posture is doing, in the only sense of "doing" that can be measured: it can place the posture inside a documented defensive repertoire, and it can name the conditions — a threat detected late and close, no retreat at hand, a web and a corner too costly to abandon — under which widows reliably stop fleeing and stand. It cannot say what she intended, because intention in the human sense is not in the evidence: two frames, eight seconds apart, record legs and their positions, not a mind. "Bravery" is not a finding the photographs can carry; it is a reading a witness brings to them — and the record keeps it labelled as exactly that, Robert's reading, standing beside the science rather than underneath it.

## 5. The scene, candidate by candidate

**Cornered, with no retreat — strengthened.** The escalation literature is conditional from end to end: widows retreat, drop, or feign while escape exists, and stand to active defence when it closes — Nelsen and colleagues got bites essentially only from grasped spiders, and the genus reviews put it in one clause, "bites occur when they cannot escape." She was out of her web on an open flat frame, the one place her retreat, her safety line, and her vibration sense do not work (Colorado State: females remain in their webs unless forced out; UC IPM: she reads the world through her web and sees very poorly). A hand arriving at close range in that place matches the documented shape of the condition that produces a stand.

**Guarding an egg sac — left standing, but weakened by the calendar.** The guarding itself is solidly documented, attendance and active defence both (Colorado State: a tending female "will defend" her sac; Arabesky and colleagues, 2025: guarding behaviour and parasitoid-killing in two *Latrodectus* species). But guarding is performed at the sac, in the web — and she was out on the frame, away from the corner where the whitish bundle sits — and late October makes a live sac the less likely of the season's two readings, as Section 3 lays out. If the bundle is a sac, she may have been moving to or from it; the frames cannot grade that.

**Startle-freeze — weakened as the whole story.** The widow's documented feint is a specific shape: drop from the web, every leg retracted tight against the body, motionless (Nelsen and colleagues' "play dead"; Vetter's thanatosis account). The second of Robert's frames shows nearly the opposite geometry — legs up, spread, and repositioned from where the first frame put them — an active set, not the death-curl. A first instant of freezing when the hand and the flash arrived remains entirely plausible; it is simply not what the pair of frames, read together, depicts.

**Territory — strengthened as the ground, weakened as the trigger.** The tenacity underneath this candidate is real and measured. The female "rarely leaves the web" (Animal Diversity Web) and remains in it "unless forced out" (Colorado State); Krugner and colleagues describe female western widows as solitary predators "with no tolerance for intruders on the webs," contesting them with vibrational rivalry signals in which the resident out-signals the introduced female roughly five to one; and in the desert widow *Latrodectus revivensis*, a mark-and-recapture study found web relocation to be mostly a juvenile event (74% of moves) carrying about a 40% chance of death on the move — a corner, for a widow, is a fortune she does not walk away from. But the defence the literature documents under the name of territory is spider-sized and web-borne, aimed at rival females through silk; nothing found describes a widow holding her ground *against a large animal* as territorial defence — against large threats the record gives flight, dropping, and feigning. Territory best explains why leaving was expensive for her. The cornered-defence literature best explains why, staying, she turned.

So the research narrows the scene to this: a long-lived, site-tenacious spider, out of the one structure her senses and defences are built around, met a threat that arrived too close for flight to be the cheap option — and did the thing her kind does in that condition, which is to stop, set her legs, and face it. What to call that is not the literature's word to choose. It is the witness's.
