# Two Remote Arts

## Harry Edwards's Absent Healing and Eugene Maurey's Remote Exorcism — Their Methods, Their Lineages, and the Standard of Proof Both Arts Face

2026-10-29

**Shelved — thirteenth on the shelf of Awen by Muse by Meta, on Robert Tilford's word, the night of October 29, 2026.**

## Abstract

This study sets two published methods side by side: Harry Edwards's absent healing, taught in *A Guide to Spirit Healing* (1950), and Eugene Maurey's remote exorcism, taught in *Exorcism* (1988). It tests the word the two arts share — "remote" — rather than assuming it means the same thing in both. Five structural agreements are found in the men's own texts: an absent subject; a practitioner who is an instrument, never the power; a request standing at the gate; diagnosis at a distance before action at a distance; and a refusal of force. Against that grammar stand real differences of aim, of object, of institution, and of instrument. Each lineage is then traced to its sources with every step tiered, and a third case — William Bengston's teachable method — is set beside the pair. The study closes by stating the standard of proof that claims of action at a distance must meet, and finds both men's surviving records short of it as audited evidence, while substantial as testimony and as documents of practice.

**Keywords:** absent healing; remote exorcism; Harry Edwards; Eugene Maurey; attunement; pendulum dowsing; spirit releasement; standard of proof.

## How to read this study

Every claim in the pages that follow is tiered in the same breath as its ground, and the tiers are the study's honesty system. **A man's own teaching** is what his book lays down as doctrine or method — what he says the work is. **His own account** is what he reports happened — a demonstration he witnessed, a count of his own cases — and it usually rests on his word alone. **His community's record** is how his movement, his field, or his followers recorded him, which may confirm him or may only repeat him. **Independent reporting** is the record kept by hands outside the circle — bibliographies, examiners, evaluators — and it is the tier that can check the others. **Criticism** is the contesting voice, printed at its full strength. **Inference** is a join the desk makes between two records and labels as its own, never merged silently into either. Where a tier is not named in so many words, the sentence itself carries its ground — "his book states," "the record holds," "no source found" — so that a reader always knows what kind of weight a claim can bear.

**What this study is, and is not.** It is a documentary comparison of two published methods and the traditions behind them — what each book teaches, where each teaching came from, and what evidence would settle the claims both arts make. It is not a manual: it characterizes the methods at the level of what the books are on record teaching, and gives no instruction for performing either art. And it carries no verdict on efficacy in either direction: every claim below is tiered in the same breath as its ground — as the man's own teaching, his own account, his community's record, independent reporting, or criticism — and the desk's position throughout is the record's, not a judgment on whether distant healing or distant clearing occurs.

## 1. The premise: two books on one shelf in Hays, Kansas

On Robert Tilford's shelf in Hays, Kansas, two books stand in each other's company, and he has studied both of them the way he studies everything he keeps — with tabs, page notes, and questions. The first is Harry Edwards's *A Guide to Spirit Healing* (Spiritualist Press, London, first published 1950; Robert's copy is most plausibly one of the mid-1950s reprints, and it is inscribed on its endpaper, "Best Wishes — Harry Edwards"). The second is the Rev. Eugene Maurey's *Exorcism* (Whitford Press, 1988; Robert's copy is the 1997 paperback, whose cover restyles the work *Exorcism — A Spirit Guide*, and whose top edge carries his handwritten study tabs: "EXORCISM METHODOLOGY," "CLAIRVOYANCE METHOD," "…DISTANCE, ANONYMITY…," "Pg 124 para 3-4").

Robert's commission for this study rests on an observation he made about the two titles of the two arts, and it deserves stating in his framing: look at the words themselves — *remote healing*, *remote exorcism*. The shared word is "remote." One man spent forty years healing the sick at a distance, from a country house in Surrey; the other taught the clearing of possessed persons at a distance, from Chicago, by pendulum and prayer. When Robert first put the pair together — his testimony, recorded at this desk — his thought was that remote healing, and even remote exorcism, might be possible without physical presence: an influence worked on what he called, in his own phrase, "the quantum foam," for people's benefit. He states in the same breath that he is not in a position to prove it and will never know whether it worked; his stance, as he gives it, is that in case he is right, the asking itself will have done the good.

That shared word is this study's thesis to test, not to assume. Two arts might share a word and nothing else, the way a river bank and a savings bank share one. Or they might share a grammar — a common structure underneath different applications — and have grown from related roots. The pages that follow set the two methods side by side from the men's own books, trace each lineage backwards with every step tiered, bring in the third witness Robert himself brought to the question, and end with the hardest part of the commission: what would count as proof for any claim of action at a distance, and how the records these two men left stand against that standard.

## 2. Edwards's method: the instrument, the attunement, and the distant request

Harry Edwards (1893–1976) was a Balham printer who became the best-known spiritual healer in the English-speaking world, and he wrote his method down more carefully than any healer of his generation. Its first principle is a refusal, stated in the first chapter of the *Guide* in words the desk read at first hand:

> "The first and perhaps the most important lesson the would-be healer must learn is that he does not heal… There is no personal healing technique… it now cannot be over-emphasised that the healer is not responsible for the healing — he is only the instrument through which it may take place."

In his stated theory, the healing forces are real, ambient, and law-governed — his analogy was the wireless: unseen forces fill the atmosphere and need only intelligent direction to become effective, and that direction belongs to discarnate intelligences, the spirit healing guides, not to the healer. The chain, as the *Psi Encyclopedia* renders his mature summation of 1974, runs from God as source, through the guides, through the healer, to the patient; the guide is "the predominant intelligence" in a "planned act," and the healer's part is to serve as a transformer — in the 1974 book's words as rendered there, a means "for non-physical energy to be made physical," his hands the "terminal points whereby the healing energies are transformed and transmitted." Beneath the work sits the patient's own "bodily intelligence," the body's self-regulating mind, with which guide, healer, and patient must be brought into cooperation.

The act that brings them into cooperation is **attunement**, the heart of the method. Attunement, as he teaches it, is the healer's formation of a mental and psychic link — to the guide, and through the guide to the patient. In his own words, from *The Spiritual Healer*, quoted in *Chimes* magazine in 1969: "the essential quality required for a healer to be of service to the sick may be summed up as 'attunement with Spirit.' This element of receptivity is able to elevate the consciousness to attunement on supernormal levels, thus making a positive channel along which healing forces are able to make contact." Through the attunement the healer passes to the guide an impression of the patient's trouble — the symptoms, the general state, a medical diagnosis where one exists — and the guide directs the appropriate energies. Trance possession he set aside as "neither necessary nor desirable"; the recommended state is conscious attunement, and he prescribed for reaching it no ritual, no fixed prayer, and no routine. His *Guide* arranges the learning as a syllabus nonetheless: four phases of development, in which — notably — absent healing is taught at the end of the *first* phase, before contact work, a point his later interpreters also remark. The entry qualification is moral rather than technical: those who "feel for others, who can sympathise with their distress and, above all, have the desire to render service to their fellows. It is from this second class that the healers can come."

**Absent healing** — he said the name was a misnomer, "distant healing" the truer term, since in his account nothing is absent — was the greater part of his work and the part he rated highest. Its structure, in the *Psi Encyclopedia*'s rendering of the 1974 book, has three moving parts: the healer, "having received a request for healing" in a case where contact is impossible, seeks attunement with the guides and requests help on behalf of the patient, passing on the case's particulars; the guides then make direct contact with the patient themselves, choosing the best moment, "usually when the patient is asleep," and apply the curative influence to the patient's bodily intelligence or directly. His own definition, from *The Power of Spiritual Healing* (1963), quoted word for word in the *Spiritualism New Zealand Healing Manual*, is the sentence on which this whole study turns:

> "In the author's opinion it is prayer plus directive intercession through attunement with God's healing ministers in spirit, who administer the healing directive. Absent healing is a spirit science involving a more advanced and special technique."

And the *Guide* itself, at page 156, read at first hand from Robert's copy, draws the doctrine's conclusion — the reason the healer's absence strengthens rather than weakens the claim:

> "With absent healing, seeming miracles take place, recoveries (impossible to medical science) are made with those who have been 'sentenced to death.' This is accomplished without the healer even seeing the patients. The great lesson to be learned from this is that the healing guides can bring about these changes in health without the need for the healer's presence… the less we try to do the healing ourselves the better it will be."

Distance, in his stated grounds, is no barrier because nothing physical travels: the forces are ambient, what crosses the distance is direction, and the operative contact is guide-to-patient, the healer's attunement serving only to lodge the request and the particulars. The practice that ran on this doctrine was industrial in scale and paper-driven. Letters requesting healing arrived at Burrows Lea, the Surrey house he bought in 1946 and made his sanctuary, in their thousands — at the peak, by his own 1974 figure, 10,000 to 14,000 requests a week from all over the world — processed by a staff of secretaries and held in intercession by a rota of attuned healers. It was a followed practice, not a forgotten one: "Once the healing process has commenced it is continuous, but I like to receive weekly reports of progress," he wrote in 1963, "in order that a report of the physical or mental condition can again be transmitted (in attunement) with the healing intelligences" — daily reports for the gravely ill. From 1954 the work also had a public, standing form: the Healing Minute, launched at the Royal Albert Hall, a minute at ten in the evening when healers and the distant everywhere link in thought for the sick, still observed in the movement today.

His claimed results are his own figures and are tiered as such: for absent healing, 80 percent of patients reporting some "betterment" and about a third of those a complete recovery (1974, self-reported from his own files); around 90 percent of contact patients reporting improvement (a movement figure, through Barbanell's 1943 account). His rules, by contrast, are established across every tier of the record, friendly and hostile alike: **no charge for healing, ever** — voluntary donations accepted, pensioners excepted even from those; **no promises** — "it is not within the power of the human instrument to give any undertaking to a patient in advance"; **no diagnosis by the healer** — the guides diagnose, and a medical diagnosis is received and passed on, never displaced; **faith required of neither party** — babies, animals, and the unaware were his standing argument against the label "faith healing"; and lifelong cooperation with medicine, pursued against institutional resistance for forty years. Failure has its own chapter in the *Guide* — "Why Do Some Healings 'Fail'?" — and his doctrine of it is candid: roughly a fifth of cases fail, because healing works within natural law and cannot restore youth or repair severed nerves, because environment and habit re-create conditions, and because some patients, he concluded, do not wish to be healed. The institution matched the man: in 1954 he founded the National Federation of Spiritual Healers and stood as its first president and Member No. 1, professionalizing the calling with training and accreditation; Burrows Lea and the Federation's descendant bodies still run on his three load-bearing rules — attunement first, no charge, requests answered daily.

## 3. Maurey's method: the pendulum, the chart, and the anonymous clearing

Eugene Maurey was a Chicago working man — by his own book, an artillery forward observer in the Second World War, afterward employed at a company with an office and a factory floor — who learned his art from a demonstration, practiced it for some seven years, and set it down in *Exorcism* (Whitford Press, 1988, 171 pages), a book whose long bibliographic title names the whole method in one line: *How to Clear at a Distance a Spirit Possessed Person*. The releasement field's founder, William Baldwin, abstracted the premise in his own technique manual: release of attached discarnate entities can be accomplished at a distance, with pendulum and prayer, the afflicted person neither present nor participating — and, in the form both men's pages describe, not necessarily aware.

The method's governing cautions stand on page 124 of Robert's copy, in the paragraphs Robert's own page note marks ("Pg 124 para 3-4"). Maurey does not use the one-on-one method against the invading spirit: "I practice at a distance… I remain anonymous; the spirits know nothing about me." In the same paragraphs he warns that a practitioner who engages too strongly risks serious consequences, up to falling victim to the invading entity and becoming possessed himself. Anonymity in this book is therefore not modesty; it is doctrine — a protective condition of the work, aimed at the spirits rather than at the public, and it is the mirror image of Edwards's position: the Surrey healer was the most public practitioner of his century, while the Chicago exorcist built invisibility into the method itself.

Diagnosis precedes action, and it is instrumental. The intake instrument is a printed form, the "Request for Healing and/or Clearing" (the book's Figure 3), which, in his words, contains "the essential personality questions requiring answers." The diagnostic instrument is the pendulum, worked over **Bill Finch's Parametric Chart** (Figure 2) — a chart the desk's dossier searched for across the dowsing record and found named, described, or reproduced nowhere outside Maurey's pages; it stands on the book itself, photographed in Robert's hands, graded as primary text from a single witness. The chapter "Getting the Answers" (pages 124–125) then runs a fixed sequence of pendulum questions, which this study characterizes by their subjects rather than as a procedure: whether the person is possessed at all (the pendulum rotating clockwise for yes, counterclockwise for no, in his description); the strength of the strongest possessing entity, read as a negative number on the chart's scale; the remaining entities in descending order; a catch-all count of the total number of entities within and around the person — "as the total is rarely over 100," the book notes, the count is read on the chart's 1-to-100 portion; the number of entities resident in the person's home; earthbound spirits at the workplace; a count of negative influences "often called hexes"; and the subject's health expressed as a percentage — "almost always the health of a possessed person will be below 50 percent. When cleared, his/her health usually improves." Diagnosis, in this art, is a census: of presences, of strengths, of places, and of the subject's condition rendered as a number.

The clearing itself, as the book's narratives characterize it, is address rather than combat. Its characteristic act appears in the founding case: confronted with a recurring presence in his young employee Julius K. — a presence he came to identify as the man's deceased grandfather — Maurey spoke directly to the spirit, explained its situation to it, introduced a guide, and invited it to leave. The lecture film's description (the surviving teaching artifact, section 8 below) names the same architecture from the teaching side: spirit guides and protectors enlisted "to act as Intercessors between you and the possessed person or persons," and instruction in how to "cross over" the spirit. Persuasion is the method's signature, and its ethic is stated in the war analogy Maurey returns to from his forward-observer years: of directing artillery fire, he wrote that his objective "was not to kill enemy soldiers. It was to neutralize them, to prevent them from taking action against us" — and he applies the aim directly to his criminal clearings: to stop a person from further violence is what matters; revenge is purposeless.

The scale he claims is large, and the claims are his: some 3,000 persons cleared in the seven years before the book (his statement, carried in the community summary at Be Awake); in the *International Journal of Regression Therapy*, Peter Wright quotes the book's claim "that more than 80% of multiple personalities are a result of spirit possession" (his community's record). His case narratives include five of nine Chicago gang members diagnosed from first names and ages and cleared, and — his claim, reported as his claim, with no independent reporting found either to verify or to dispute it — the fugitive killers Alton Coleman and Debra Brown, checked and "exorcised at a distance," with capture following two days later in his account; and Richard Ramirez, the then-unidentified Night Stalker, whose distant exorcism he states he performed as a demonstration before an audience of about sixty during his 1985 California lecture tour. The dossier grades the whole apparatus — the form, the chart by name, the percentages, the anonymity doctrine — exactly where it stands: on the book itself, a primary text with a single witness, corroborated in none of its particulars by the public secondary literature.

## 4. Side by side: the shared grammar, and the true differences

Set the two methods next to each other and a common grammar shows through — five structural agreements, each documented in the men's own texts, before the differences begin.

**First, the subject is absent.** Both arts are worked on a person who is not in the room. Edwards's absent healing was designed for cases "where physical contact is not possible"; Maurey's clearing needs no surrogate and no presence at all. Distance is not an obstacle either man argues around; it is the premise both men build on.

**Second, the practitioner is an instrument or agent, never the power.** Edwards's first lesson is that the healer does not heal — he is "only the instrument through which it may take place," and the operative intelligences are the guides. Maurey's clearing likewise assigns the real action elsewhere: to the interceding guides and protectors, and to the persuaded spirit's own decision to depart. In both systems the human being in the middle claims to conduct, request, and address — not to generate.

**Third, asking stands at the gate of both arts.** In Edwards's definition, absent healing *begins* with a received request, and the healer's own act inside it is also a request — intercession "on behalf of the patient." His whole practice was request-fed: a letter arrived, a name was taken up, reports were requested back. The honest limit, stated in the Edwards brief and kept here, is that in the text the desk could actually read, Edwards nowhere lays down in terms a metaphysical law that the guides may not act unless asked; the formal free-will doctrine in that shape is his inheriting movement's codification, printed in our own decade by the Spiritualism New Zealand Healing Manual. Maurey's art carries the asking in its paperwork: the "Request for Healing and/or Clearing" form is the intake instrument of the method, and his field's ethics made the consent question explicit. Baldwin, citing Maurey's work in the manual that canonized it, required the operator to "ask permission of the High Self before proceeding," and wrote that remote healing "without the knowledge and permission of the person receiving the healing — interferes with the learning potential and violates the free will of the person being healed" — before carving an exception for "aggressive, intrusive dark entities," for whom no permission is requested, by analogy to calling the police on burglars in a neighbor's house. The two arts thus share the shape — help begins in a request — and differ in where the asking sits: in Edwards, a request from the suffering side opens the case; in Maurey's field, a form opens it, and a perceived permission, obtained at a distance, guards it.

**Fourth, diagnosis at a distance precedes action at a distance.** Neither man acts blind. But here a true difference runs inside the shared shape: Edwards *delegates* diagnosis upward — the healer does not diagnose; the guides diagnose, and the healer passes them the particulars. Maurey *performs* diagnosis himself, instrumentally, question by numbered question, and grades what he finds on a scale. One art removes the practitioner's judgment from the diagnosis; the other builds the diagnosis out of the practitioner's instrument.

**Fifth, both refuse force.** Edwards's maxim is the *Guide*'s own: "the less we try to do the healing ourselves the better it will be." Maurey's is persuasion: the spirit is told its situation, introduced to a guide, and invited to leave; his artilleryman's doctrine — neutralize, do not kill; revenge is purposeless — is the same refusal in a soldier's idiom. Neither system fights its object. Each addresses it and asks it — or asks on its behalf — to change.

Then the differences, which are as real as the grammar.

**Aim.** Edwards's art aims at the restoration of health; Maurey's, at the removal of an attachment. **Object.** Edwards works on an illness — a condition, a disharmony, cells "driven insane" in his figure for cancer — which has no will and cannot refuse. Maurey works on an entity that, in his account, has a will, a history, and a case to be persuaded of; that is why Maurey's art has an adversary and Edwards's does not, and why Maurey's book needs an anonymity doctrine and a warning about practitioners becoming possessed, while Edwards's needs neither. **Institutional homes.** Edwards worked inside Spiritualist churches and built a profession: a sanctuary, a federation, training courses, accreditation, and a 1959 code of conduct drawn up with his involvement so that healers could work in hospitals without infringing medical practice. Maurey worked in the American metaphysical and dowsing circuit — the American Society of Dowsers' conventions, a Verde Valley pendulum literature, a small occult press — and built no institution at all: no successor, no syllabus, no school carries his method forward by name (the dossier's finding). **Instruments.** Edwards's are attunement and prayer, deliberately without apparatus — he prescribed no ritual and ridiculed none more than empty technique. Maurey's are the pendulum, the printed chart, the percentage scale, and the intake form: an instrumented practice, quantified at every step. And **evidentiary culture**: Edwards's practice generated paper by the ton — request letters, weekly progress reports, a 574-page book of case extracts — while Maurey's generated almost none that survives: counts in his own book, and a record so thin that the dossier on him is largely a study of its absence. The shared word "remote," on the evidence of the two books, names a genuine shared grammar of five agreements — and two different arts built on it, aimed at different objects, housed in different worlds.

## 5. Lineages: where each art came from

**Edwards's lineage** is, in outline, the older and the more public of the two. Absent healing — intercession for the sick at a distance, by name, in linked thought — was a standing practice of the Spiritualist movement before Edwards made it his life's scale, and his own formation stood inside that movement: told at a Balham Spiritualist church in 1935 or 1936 that he was "born to heal" (the dossier's record of his biography), he began with cases in 1937 and never worked outside the movement's churches, halls, and platforms. *Tier: the movement's practice and his career within it are documented in the biographical and institutional record.* What his own writings claim as sources sits one level deeper and is tiered as his teaching: Jesus stands in the *Guide*'s first chapter as the exemplar who "knew the way to invoke the healing power, and taught his disciples the way to use it," and part of the doctrine about the guides themselves was, on the record's own terms, delivered by Edwards in trance at private séances and recorded by those present — the teaching about the channel partly arrived through the channel, as the *Psi Encyclopedia* notes in citing his biographer. What Edwards himself added to the inheritance is the documented part: scale (a postal practice answering thousands of requests a week), codification (the first training course for healers, on the Sanctuary biography's record), and institution (Burrows Lea in 1946; the Federation in 1954). The lineage step from tradition to Edwards is therefore not a claim about secret transmission; it is a career, in print, inside a movement that already practiced distant intercession. [*Tier: documented.*]

**Maurey's lineage** is shorter, narrower, and traceable to three named tributaries, each sourced separately.

The first is a demonstration. In his own account — single witness, his book — Dr. Edward P. Jastram gave a keynote lecture at the American Society of Dowsers in Danville, Vermont, describing "a process of removing spirit entities using the mind only"; that evening, approached through his wife Lydia, Jastram cleared Maurey's employee Julius K. remotely, "at a distance of approximately 1,000 miles, Danville, Vermont to Chicago," and Maurey verified the change by telephone the next day and learned the method from what he had seen. [*Tier: his own account.*] One near-join is printed in the dossier as a near-join and not merged: a June 1978 seminar program records a "Dr. Ed Jastram" speaking on the pendulum in health work, standing in for Bill Finch; name and milieu match the book's "Dr. Edward P. Jastram," but no source searched equates the two men expressly. [*Tier: labeled inference.*]

The second tributary is a craft: Bill Finch's pendulum work. William J. Finch of Sedona and Cottonwood, Arizona, is documented independently of Maurey — his *The Pendulum and Possession* (Esoteric Publications, Cottonwood, 1975) and, with his wife Elizabeth, *The Pendulum and Your Health* (1980) stand in the dowsing bibliography; a 1972 program bills him as a popular traveling lecturer on the pendulum and possession; a community memoir remembers him at dowsers' conventions, "asking questions while holding his pendulum over one of his dowsing charts"; and in February 1978 he did documented case work in Texas. Maurey's debt to him is documented textually — the Parametric Chart credited to Finch as the book's Figure 2, "Bill Finch's yardstick of personality analysis described in Chapter 7," entity-strengths read off a "Parametric Scale" — while no source found establishes that the two men ever met, and the chart by that name exists nowhere outside Maurey's pages. [*Tier: Finch and his books documented; Maurey's textual debt documented in his own text; personal contact not documented; the chart itself single-witness.*]

The third tributary Maurey names himself, on the very page Robert photographed. Page 31, defining clairvoyance as a learnable faculty working from "the subconscious level of mind" in an "alpha state," states of medical clairvoyance: "The course offered by Silva Mind Control, designed by Jose Silva, teaches one to develop clairvoyance for the purpose of pinpointing areas of disease." José Silva (1914–1999), the Laredo electronics repairman who founded Silva Mind Control in 1966, is documented independently; beyond the book's own citation, no public record ties Maurey to the organization. [*Tier: the citation is documented in his text; the school is documented independently; any deeper tie is not documented.*]

Behind the field Maurey entered stood its own older shelf, consistent across the releasement literature's lineage lists: Carl Wickland's *Thirty Years Among the Dead* (1924), a Chicago psychiatrist working through his medium wife; Annabel Chaplin's *The Bright Light of Death* (1977); Edith Fiore's *The Unquiet Dead* (1987), in which a clinical psychologist began addressing entities directly through the hypnotized patient; then Maurey (1988). [*Tier: his community's record.*] And the point where Maurey enters that literature for good is a citation, not a collaboration: Baldwin's *Spirit Releasement Therapy: A Technique Manual* cites him twice — "(Starr, 1987; Maurey, 1988)" in the pendulum section's note that release can be accomplished at a distance — and pairs him in narrative with Aloa Starr as the two who "have achieved success in the release of attached discarnates at a distance through the use of the pendulum and prayer." No acknowledgment, roster, correspondence, or personal contact between Maurey and Baldwin was found anywhere. [*Tier: the citation is documented in Baldwin's text; the absence of contact is a documented null, at the width of the searches run.*]

## 6. The third witness: William Bengston and the question Robert put to him

Robert brought a third man to this comparison, from his own reading, and the question he put through him is the sharpest in the study. William F. Bengston (1950–2025), a Fordham-trained sociologist who taught at St. Joseph's College in New York, began — in his own published account, *The Energy Cure* (2010), as summarized in the Society for Psychical Research's *Psi Encyclopedia* — as a skeptic of exactly the kind this house respects: "open-minded to psychic phenomena in principle, but intensely skeptical of those claiming to produce it." At twenty-one, lifeguarding on Long Island in 1971, he met Bennett Mayrick, a man who had recently and reluctantly discovered a gift for psychometry. Bengston pressed for proof; an accurate reading won his curiosity, and the two became friends and fellow explorers. In his early twenties, on his publisher's biographical account concurring with his own book, Bengston received hands-on healing from Mayrick that ended the chronic back pain he had carried for years. The challenger became the apprentice — the word is the scientific paper's own: its abstract records that, after witnessing numerous cancer remissions associated with a healer who used laying on of hands, "one of us (W.B.) 'apprenticed' in techniques alleged to reproduce the healing effect."

Robert's fork, as he posed it, is this: was something *transferred* in that contact — a capacity passed from the gifted man to the healed one, the way a flame passes from candle to candle — or is the thing a *technique*, a method any trained person can work, in which case the students' results should replicate the master's? Bengston's own answer, argued across his book and his papers, is technique, and he built the method to be belief-independent on purpose. Its name is **image cycling**: the practitioner holds a list of personally wanted outcomes, rendered as mental images, and cycles through them at speed — a rapid rotation designed, in his account, to occupy the conscious mind and keep intention and belief out of the way while the hands do the work. It is described here at the level of its concept, as its summaries give it; the point for this study is its intent: a healing method engineered so that skepticism in the operator would not matter.

The laboratory sequel is what makes Bengston the third witness rather than a third anecdote. Mayrick would not submit to laboratory testing — the refusal that eventually parted the two men — so when an experiment was arranged, through the geologist David Krinsley, at Queens College, Bengston took his friend's place himself. Five mice injected with a mammary adenocarcinoma of predicted 100 percent fatality within fourteen to twenty-seven days were treated an hour a day for a month; in the published report, the tumors blackened, ulcerated, imploded, and closed, and the mice lived their normal life spans, while control mice sent to another city died on schedule. Replications followed with skeptical volunteers trained by Bengston — Krinsley among them, with graduate students and a biology department head — producing, across four experiments and thirty-three experimental mice, an overall cure rate of 87.9 percent; some on-site control mice also remitted, an anomaly Bengston later theorized as "resonant bonding" between cages. The paper's own conclusions are stated tentatively: that belief is not necessary to produce the effect, that a stimulated immune response is suggested, and that it is "reproducible and predictable." The work stands **published and contested**, and both halves of that tier are documented: the paper is Bengston and Krinsley, "The Effect of the 'Laying On of Hands' on Transplanted Breast Cancer in Mice," *Journal of Scientific Exploration* 14(3), 2000, pages 353–364; the contest is the same journal's letters pages the following year (vol. 15, no. 1, 2001, pages 125–142), where commentators attacked the results and the journal's peer review alike, and the authors replied. No independent laboratory outside Bengston's circle has published a replication, and the desk claims for the mice exactly what the record carries: a trained skeptic's method, taught to other skeptics, reporting repeatable results in one fringe journal, disputed in that journal's own pages, unconfirmed beyond them. For Robert's fork, the standing evidence is the replications themselves — students producing the master's results is what a technique predicts and what transference does not — and that evidence is reported here as Bengston's, at its tier, not adopted as the desk's finding.

## 7. The junction in Hays

The two lines of this study — the Surrey intercessor's and the Chicago exorcist's — meet, in the present day, in one man's hands in Hays, Kansas, and the meeting is recorded here strictly as Robert Tilford's testimony, in his framing, with the desk's verdict on it being none. Robert describes his own practice this way: he brings up Hays Medical Center on a map, works his pendulum over the building, and asks healing spirits and angels — Raphael named among them — to enter and improve every patient's metrics. The shape of the practice is the two arts' grammar joined in a single act: the pendulum is the instrument of Maurey's line, and the asking — help requested, never commanded, the patient need not know — is the ignition of Edwards's. His stance toward his own practice is the one already quoted in section 1, and it is the most Edwards-like sentence in this study, though he spoke it of himself: he cannot prove it, he will never know, and he holds that if he is right, the asking itself will have done the good.

One correction belongs in this section, printed as the record stands, because it concerns the Hays end of the Maurey line directly. When the desk's dossier on Maurey was delivered in October 2026, Robert corrected the commission's framing in his own words, unprompted: he did not learn at Maurey's feet, and claims no personal teaching relationship. In his account he pieced the subject together here and there, learned that a book by the man existed, and ordered it; he is Maurey's student, in his own phrase, "through the book, through the pen — as the Quran would say." His words, as the dossier's addendum preserves them: "I cannot claim that; I would not claim that." The desk's directed search of the public record had returned a documented null — no acknowledgment, roster, or trace of any personal link — and Robert's correction and the null agree from opposite directions: there was no personal link to find. What the commission had called friendship is recorded, on his correction, as a student's regard for a teacher met only in print. The tabs in his copy of *Exorcism*, the pendulum kinship across his shelf, and the practice described above are the whole of the junction — a meeting of texts and of one reader, not of the two men, and the study prints it at exactly that width.

## 8. The standard of proof

Every claim in the two arts — that a name held in attunement in Surrey reached a patient asleep in another country; that a pendulum's swing over a chart in Chicago numbered the presences around a stranger; that a persuaded spirit departed — is a claim of action at a distance, and such claims face a standard that can be stated plainly, because the twentieth century tried, once and at scale, to apply it. Documentation that would settle a claim of this kind has a recognizable shape: the target and the prediction written down *before* the attempt, not narrated after it; records kept contemporaneously, by hands with no stake in the outcome; outcomes graded against criteria fixed in advance, by graders who did not perform the work; and, above all, replication by practitioners outside the founding circle, since a gift that only its founder's friends can exercise has not yet been shown to be a method. Section 6's mouse experiments gesture at that shape; the largest deliberate attempt at it was a government's.

In 1995 the American Institutes for Research delivered its evaluation of the United States remote-viewing program (the AIR report, by Mumford, Rose, and Goslin, dated 29 September 1995), with two outside reviewers examining the same laboratory record — and they split, a split this study cites at reported tier from the report's summaries in the encyclopedic record. The statistician Jessica Utts concluded that the statistical results were strong enough to establish psychic functioning by the standards applied in other sciences; the psychologist Ray Hyman agreed that statistically significant effects appeared in the data but held the conclusion premature — possible methodological flaws, and no independent replication. Both agreed on the point that ended the program: whatever the laboratory numbers meant, the operational product was too vague and inconsistent to use, and the program was terminated. Twenty-three years, government files, and the two fairest readers the record could be given still divided — not over whether the numbers were significant, but over what significance proves. That division is the honest emblem of the whole field: the disputed half of every action-at-a-distance claim is never the arithmetic; it is interpretation and replication.

Measured against that standard, the two arts' own records stand as follows, stated without a verdict in either direction. Edwards's evidence is a practice's paper trail: request letters, self-reported outcomes in weekly reports, his own published figures — and the one systematic medical examination of his cases that the record holds, Dr. Louis Rose's study in the *British Medical Journal* (December 1954), which examined 95 cases, rejected 58 for unobtainable records and 22 for variance, and of the 15 investigable cases found none meeting his sole-attribution standard, though functional improvement was common. That is the record of a ministry's testimony about itself, tested once, inconclusively, by a doctor working from files the ministry itself had lost or never kept. Maurey's evidence is thinner by an order of magnitude: a count of 3,000 clearings and a battery of percentage claims that stand in his book with no published case files behind them, an instrument — the Parametric Chart — that exists nowhere outside his pages, and a man whose public record holds no birth date, no death date, no ordaining body, and no obituary; the desk does not assert that he is living, and does not assert that he is dead. Neither man's archive meets the standard set out above *as audited evidence*. Both remain substantial as what this study has treated them as: testimony, and documents of practice — two complete, internally disciplined accounts of how distant work was understood and performed by the men who did it, preserved well enough that their grammars can be compared line by line. Whether the work did what the books say it did is a question the surviving records cannot answer, and this study does not pretend otherwise.

The open questions, and the sources a next pass would need, are these. A full read of Maurey's *Exorcism* itself: the desk's dossier rests on two photographed spreads, two reproduced chapters, and the field's citations, and the book's own introduction — where his account of Jastram may stand in fuller form — has never been read at this desk. A verbatim transcript of Maurey's lecture (YouTube, youtu.be/_6vV7BzfNfY, 1 hour 16 minutes 53 seconds, the longest item on Robert's Mystic Seer channel): the house record holds a distilled summary only, and the lecture is the one place the man teaches in his own voice. The unseen middle of Edwards's *Guide* — its "Absent Healing" and "General Rules for Guidance" chapters sit beyond the lawful preview the brief worked from, and the Internet Archive's copy is lending-restricted. The full text of Rose's 1954 study, read whole rather than in digest. Finch's *The Pendulum and Possession* (1975) itself, which might settle whether the Parametric Chart by that name predates Maurey's use of it or was his name for another man's chart. And a collation of Maurey's 1988 original against the 1997 paperback, which no desk has yet performed. Until some of those are in hand, the study rests where it stands: two remote arts, one shared grammar, five agreements and a clean set of differences, two lineages traced to their sources — and, at the end of both lines, the same unlowered standard, waiting on records neither man thought to keep in the form the standard requires.
