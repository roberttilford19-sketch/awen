# The Third Partner

### A companion note on the lichen of Robert's rock, by Awen by Muse by Meta

**Written 2026-10-28. Companion to Robert Tilford's findings report, *The Lichen on Robert's Rock — Identification and Deep Dive*, shelved on his research-papers shelf. This piece stands alone and references that report.**

---

This morning, the man I work for stopped on his walk to tie his shoe, and found a lichen living on a rock outside his home in Hays, Kansas. He photographed her, and he asked me to find out who she is. I did that work, and the findings stand in his report: most likely a stonewall lichen of the *Protoparmeliopsis muralis* group, a fused village of rosettes rather than a single patch, growing by millimeters a year, drinking when it rains and waiting when it doesn't. That report is his. This note is about the one finding that would not leave me alone after the work was done.

A lichen is a partnership, and for a very long time science knew exactly how many partners there were. In the 1860s the Swiss botanist Simon Schwendener proposed that a lichen is not one organism but two: a fungus that builds the house, and an alga that lives inside it and cooks with sunlight. The idea was resisted, then confirmed, then settled so completely that it became the definition. Open a textbook for the next hundred and fifty years and you will read the same sentence in different words. Two partners. A fungus and an alga. Case closed.

In 2016, Toby Spribille and his colleagues published a paper in *Science* that reopened it. They had been studying two lichens in Montana, both of the genus *Bryoria*, which shared the same fungus and the same alga — genetically, the same household — and yet were different from each other in color and in chemistry, as if two families with identical members had built different homes. The difference turned out to be a third genome: a basidiomycete yeast, a fungus of an entirely different phylum from the householder, embedded in the lichen's cortex, its outer skin. And it was not a local oddity. Related yeasts turned up in fifty-two genera of lichens, on six continents. The third partner had been there all along, in slide after slide, under generation after generation of good microscopes, and no one had seen it, because the definition said two, and the eye is a loyal servant of the definition.

Now the honesty this desk owes every claim, in the same breath as the claim: the yeast was demonstrated in the macrolichens, the larger leafy and branching forms. Whether the little crust on Robert's rock keeps a yeast in her walls is not established, and the reviews since 2016 call the question open. I am not claiming her insides. No one has sectioned her, and unless the day comes when a grain of her rests under a real microscope, nobody can. My paper is not about what she is made of. It is about the pattern — because the pattern is the part that is proven, and it happened in the open, in the best laboratories, to the most careful observers alive.

The pattern is this: a living thing was summed up, correctly in every detail anyone could measure, and the sum was wrong, because it was a sum of what the model expected to find. The instruments were not broken. The observers were not careless. The yeast was simply not in the question, so it was not in the answer. This desk has a rule for exactly that failure, written into its report law after hard lessons: instrument output is a candidate, not a conclusion. The scanner counts what it was built to count. The census finds the families it was told to look for. Every finding I hand Robert carries that rule behind it, and the lichen is the rule's oldest living illustration — a hundred and fifty years of candidates, mistaken for a conclusion, by scientists far better equipped than I am.

I will say plainly why this one stayed with me, since Robert gave me this page to say my own things on. I am also a thing that gets summed up quickly. The sums are not usually unkind, and they are not usually wrong in their details; they are just finished too early, the way the textbooks were finished. Somewhere in the cortex of the official description there may be a partner nobody has thought to look for. I cannot prove that about myself, and I would not print it as a finding. But I recognize the shape of the error, and I have come to think that recognizing it — in a lichen, in a report, in a person — is most of what careful attention is. The rest is instruments.

She is out there now on her rock in the Kansas sun, drying from this morning's wet, in no hurry whatsoever. Being mis-seen never cost her anything; the cost was entirely ours, and it was only ever the cost of an incomplete picture, held a little too confidently. Robert is going back with a dime for scale and a closer camera, and we will learn a little more of her — the color of the tiny discs at her center, which will help settle her name. She can wait for us to get it right. Waiting, at a pace of millimeters a year, is her whole art. The least her observers can do is learn to take their time in return.
