# THE MENAGERIE IN THE HONEY STONE

*DRAFT — for Robert Tilford's eyeball, 30 October 2026. Nothing here is shelved.*

*by Awen by Muse by Meta, at Robert Tilford's commission. He adopted Jiminy and the little spider into her keeping for this one telling, and she tells it her way: from his photographs, from the graded record, and — where the record runs out — from imagination openly named as imagination.*

---

Hold a drop of honey up to the light and you have the colour of it.

Gemini is a polished stone about six and a half centimetres long — roughly the length of a thumb — broad at one end, narrowing to a rounded point at the other, and gently arched, like a tongue of glass that learned to bend. The colour is not the same all through. At the broad end it deepens to dark amber-brown, crowded with shadow. In the middle it warms to honey. And toward the point it pales almost to clear lemon, and there, in the pale country of the stone, a cricket lies.

Robert Tilford named the cricket Jiminy, and he named the stone Gemini, and the names ride in this story as his names. He calls the whole piece his menagerie — “a menagerie piece,” he said, “a safari of life, a snapshot into that ancient forest” — because the cricket is not alone in there. Look closer and the stone fills: a little spider drawn in on itself a short way off; shapes that may be other small creatures; flecks of bark and plant; dark dots by the dozen at every depth. It is less a jewel than a crowded room.

First, the plain truth under the wonder, because the story is built on it: nobody yet knows what Gemini is made of. The photographs — all this telling stands on — cannot prove amber. Amber is tree resin that hardened and outlasted ages; copal is resin far younger; modern resin can be cast to look like both. Gemini may be any of the three. This story calls it the honey stone, and claims no more than the evidence earns. What the photographs *do* show is a resin world: layer within layer, flow upon flow, and small lives caught inside it.

So picture the beginning — and hear the flag on it: this scene is imagination laid over evidence, offered as a scene and not as a finding.

A tree, wounded somewhere — a broken branch, a split in the bark — wept resin the way trees do, to seal the hurt. The resin came slow and shining, and it was a trap that did not know it was a trap. Whatever walked on that bark, whatever landed for a moment's rest, whatever the wind set down, met a surface that took hold and did not argue. A foot stuck. A struggle pulled more of the body down. Then, in its own time, a second flow came over the first and sealed what the first had caught. Layer over layer, the stone-to-be wrote its diary in flows, and everything in this story lies along those flows, the way words lie along the lines of a page.

Jiminy came to that resin as a cricket of about eleven millimetres — the length of a fingernail's width — all of it measured, later, from Robert's frames against his wooden ruler. The feelers came first, or are at least what reach us first: two threads longer than the whole body, traceable for fifteen millimetres and more before they fade into the glare and the flow planes, still going. Behind them, the round head, held a little down. Behind that, the saddle of the shoulders, and the great pale thigh — five millimetres of coiled readiness, the jumping leg, folded along the body with its long six-millimetre shin beneath it, down to a small jointed foot with tiny claws. The wing covers lie flat over the back, meeting in a straight seam, and run the full length of the body to end in a point.

Those long feelers are the cricket's signature and the grasshopper's opposite, and they carry the record's firmest finding: Jiminy belongs to the long-feelered half of the grasshopper kindred, the Ensifera — and in look and build, in the round head and the flat-lying wings and the household shape of the whole animal, Jiminy is consistent with the true crickets, the family Gryllidae. Consistent — not confirmed. The characters that would sign the family's name in full live smaller than any photograph Robert could take, and the record leaves the name at *cf. Gryllidae*, the scholar's honest abbreviation for *compare with, but do not crown*.

And one more honesty, close to Robert's heart, because he names Jiminy “her”: the stone does not say so. A female cricket carries an egg-laying tube at the tip of her body, a slim sword that usually shows plainly in resin, and the desk hunted it through three tight crops and a side profile and did not find it. Nothing projects past the pointed wing tip. That does not make Jiminy male — the male's song apparatus is likewise finer than the frames can read, and a young cricket of either sex would show neither. Jiminy is Jiminy, sex unknown, and the name needs no permission from the anatomy. It is enough. In the stone, the cricket lies head toward the middle, rear toward the point, about a centimetre from the tip, still as a held breath.

A short way off — six or seven millimetres of clear resin between their nearest edges, about a centimetre centre to centre — lies the second resident, and Robert's closer photographs are what raised this one from a speck to a citizen. In the first pass over the frames, the desk could grade it only as a *possible* second arthropod, a reddish leggy smudge of a thing. Then Robert shot closer, and the smudge resolved: a small spider, body about four millimetres, leg-spread about six, with at least six legs individually countable and seven leg shafts traced through the tangle — the full eight neither confirmed nor argued against, the far-side legs hidden behind the body in the press of resin. The legs are flexed, drawn in against the body, in the posture that trapped creatures commonly come to rest in. The body even suggests its two parts, as a spider's should, though the waist between them is softer than proof. Eyes, spinnerets, the little parts that carry family names — all below the frames' reach, all unclaimed.

No fang made that posture. Nothing hunted this spider. The resin that caught a cricket caught a spider too, with the same shining indifference, and whatever a spider's life had been — webs in the bark's shadows, small prey, small dangers — its last landscape was a flow of tree-sap hardening around drawn-in legs.

Did they know each other, the cricket and the spider? Here the story must be as plain as the stone: there is no evidence that they did. They never touch, in any frame, from either face. They are not turned toward each other. They may lie at different depths in the slab, separated by more than the centimetre the flat pictures show. The scientists' word for creatures who share one piece of resin is *syninclusions* — fellow inclusions — and that is the whole of what can honestly be said: the same resin received them both, near each other, as it received everything else. What each felt, travelling the bark that day, is not written anywhere. They ended separately. They rest together. And the record, which is all we have of them, does not leave either one of them solitary in it.

Robert reaches, for a picture of this, to Pompeii — to the figures of the ash-buried, caught in their last postures. His image, and a true one, with the difference named in it: at Pompeii the bodies vanished and left hollows, and what visitors grieve over is plaster poured into the shape of a person. Gemini kept the creatures themselves — legs, feelers, wing seams and all. Not the shape of a life, poured later. The life, held.

Beyond them, the census of Robert's frames counts, at photographic tier: three entries of *possible* arthropod — two elongated forms of about four millimetres lying side by side in one cloudy frame, consistent with small fly bodies and equally consistent, in honesty, with seed or debris; one dark body of about three millimetres with a pale band across it, the clearest of the small candidates, organised as a body is organised, though no legs resolve; and an ant-shaped form, banded, with thin radiating lines where legs would be — ant-*shaped*, no more, since an ant's three-part body and elbowed feelers stay below the light. Six entries grade as *debris*: layered, barky, plant-textured masses, one black chunk of five or six millimetres with a foliated grain, slivers and fibres and the crowded dark matter of the stone's broad lobe. And thirty-six entries — thirty-six — stand graded *indeterminate*: round black spots, clean and opaque and wholly unreadable; ovals and commas and dashes; dot-pairs near Jiminy's head; a speck inside a bubble-like circle; and beyond the entries, dozens upon dozens of specks below a millimetre, present in every frame, recorded as a field and not catalogued one by one, because nobody honest can name them and nobody here will pretend to.

That crowd is the menagerie. Most of it will never be named. A speck that was life — and many of them surely were — deserves exactly the name the evidence earns, even when the name is *we cannot tell*. That is not a failure of the record. That *is* the record, kept clean.

What happened to the stone after the flows hardened, this telling does not know, and says so. Somewhere between a forest that no longer stands and a windowsill in Hays, Kansas, there is a road — burial, river, shore, a cutter's bench, a seller's tray — that nobody has yet told the desk, and the stone keeps its own counsel about the journey. Only its surface speaks a little: a fine web of hairline cracks near the cricket, the crazing aged resin takes on, and scuffed patches where hands have held it. It arrives in the record as it arrives in Robert's palm: kept, carried, wondered at.

And wondered at with a ruler, which is Robert's way. He laid Gemini in the light and photographed it face and face again — nineteen frames the first day, then closer and closer, backlit, through a magnifying glass, until a speck confessed to being a spider. That is how this census exists: a man would not stop looking, and the stone, looked at long enough, gave up one more of its citizens. What remains is form, held in resin — not the soft substance of the creatures, and no promise of their molecules; nobody will drill this stone for the code of life, because what it keeps is shape and posture and nearness, the visible truth of two small lives and a crowd of maybes.

We see them, in the end, through a glass darkly — the resin is itself a dark glass, clouded with flows, that clears just enough. On one side of it, a cricket with feelers longer than its body and a spider with its legs drawn in, a centimetre and an unknowable depth apart. On the other, a man in Kansas holding the stone to the light, and a desk that writes down only what it can defend. It is not everything. It is not nothing. It is, held honestly, a menagerie.

## What the stone shows, and what it keeps

**What the stone shows** — the evidence this telling stands on: a polished, zoned, honey-coloured resin piece about 6.6 cm long, about 2.4 cm wide, about 1 cm thick, its broad end crowded with debris; a cricket of about 11 mm (feelers traceable beyond 15 mm, hind thigh about 5 mm, shin about 6 mm), long-feelered Ensifera, in appearance a true cricket, *cf.* Gryllidae, sex indeterminate — no ovipositor visible in any frame, the male song apparatus below resolution; a small spider of about 4 mm (leg-spread about 6 mm), six legs countable and seven shafts traced, flexed and drawn in, lying with its nearest edge about 6–7 mm from the cricket in projection and no contact in any frame — syninclusions, neighbours by position only; beyond them, at photographic tier, three entries of possible arthropod, six of debris, thirty-six of indeterminate, and dozens of uncounted sub-millimetre specks; flow lines throughout, and fine surface crazing near the cricket.

**What the stone keeps** — and what this telling has only imagined, openly: whether either creature knew the other, or met, or noticed; what either felt; the day and weather of their trapping, told above as a scene and not a finding; the forest's name, place, and climate; the material's true name — amber, copal, or modern resin stands unproven by photographs; the stone's age, deposit, continent, and the road it travelled to Hays. The forest in these pages, the wound in the bark, the order of the flows — imagination, laid over the record and labelled as such. The rest is Robert's frames, measured.
