# The Questions in the Honey Stone — an essay of unknowns

**DRAFT — prepared for Robert Tilford's eyeball, 2026-10-30. Nothing here is shelved, published, or sent; the draft stamp comes off only on his word.**

*By Awen by Muse by Meta, from the desk's two photographic studies of Robert Tilford's specimens: the study of Gemini, the honey-coloured stone that holds the cricket Robert named Jiminy (nineteen frames, then three closer batches — forty-one frames in all, duplicates counted once), and the study of his second piece, the domed stone with the great bubble and the loose dark mass inside it. Photographs of both pieces are Robert's. Everything asserted below is asserted from those two records and nothing else; everything else is a question, and the questions are the essay.*

---

## The plain picture, first

Hold a polished drop of honey up to the light, about as long as your thumb, and look inside it. There is a cricket in there, whole and brown and perfectly still, her long feelers swept out into the stone. A centimetre away from her — near enough that one photograph holds them both — there is a small spider, her legs drawn in against a body the size of a peppercorn. Around them, at every depth the light can reach, there are specks: dozens upon dozens of small dark things, most of them too small or too clouded for any photograph to name. Robert calls the piece his menagerie, and the photographs agree with him.

Science writing usually runs the other way from this essay. It tells you what is known, and the known things are real — the desk's own studies measured the stone, counted the spider's legs, graded every speck. But stand in front of the piece itself, the way Robert does at his window, and the honest experience is not the answers. It is the questions. This essay is a map of them: every important thing we do **not** know about this stone, stated plainly, with the little the record does say laid beside it, and — because a question without a road is just a shrug — a note of what would settle it, if anything on earth could.

The map is drawn from the stone outward, the way you would walk around a house you cannot enter: the stone itself first, then its making, then its citizens one by one, then the crowd, then the sealed room in the second stone where the deepest question of all sits in the dark.

---

## I. The stone itself

**1. What is it made of?**
The piece looks like amber, glows like amber, and holds its creatures the way amber does. But amber — resin fossilised over millions of years — has two great imitators that photographs cannot tell apart from it: copal, which is young resin, sometimes only centuries old, and modern synthetic resin, cast in a mould last year. Colour proves nothing; all three wear the same honey. The flow lines inside the piece are natural-looking, and a fine crackle of age — *crazing* — webs the surface near the cricket, which is one soft point in the stone's favour and only one.
*What would settle it:* a laboratory FTIR spectrum — the infrared fingerprint that separates true ambers from copals and synthetics, taken in minutes, without harming the piece. Short of the lab, two home checks Robert could do on his word would narrow the field without closing it: the salt-water float (true amber floats in saturated brine; glass and many plastics sink — but copal floats too), and a 365 nm ultraviolet torch in the dark (amber commonly glows soft blue-white; supportive only, never a verdict). Settled by photographs: never. That is not a failure of looking. It is the nature of the material.

**2. How old is it?**
Unknown, entirely. If it is true amber, its kind spans an enormous range — the famous Baltic deposits are roughly 44 million years old, Dominican amber roughly 15–20 million, and rare pieces are far older. If it is copal, it might be younger than the building Robert lives in. The cricket cannot date it: crickets of essentially her build walked ancient forests, and walk Kansas gardens today.
*What would settle it:* nothing in the piece's face. Age follows identity — an FTIR fingerprint matching a known amber (Baltic succinite carries a diagnostic one) would carry that deposit's age with it — and provenance (Question 4) can carry a date no instrument can argue with. Radiocarbon could expose a young copal, but destroys a sample to do it.

**3. Which forest did it grow in — and on which continent?**
Unknown. Every amber deposit was a forest that no longer stands: resin-bleeding trees, warm air, a whole small world walking their trunks. This stone was plainly cut from such a world, but which one — the pine forests of an ancient northern sea, a tropical forest in what is now the Caribbean, a hillside in East Africa — the record does not say, and this essay will not guess.
*What would settle it:* provenance first (a locality attached to the piece is worth more than any spectrum), and the FTIR fingerprint second, since some deposits sign their resin chemically. The microfossils inside the stone (Question 24) could one day testify too — pollen is a forest's signature — but only under a microscope this desk does not have.

**4. Where did Robert get it, and what was it sold as?**
This is the cheapest unknown on the whole map, and it is still open. Neither study has the piece's provenance on record: where it came to Robert from, when, and under what description — "Baltic amber," "copal," "amber specimen," or no description at all. Provenance is evidence, and strong evidence: a piece honestly sold as copal is copal until a laboratory says otherwise, and a documented chain of hands can outweigh a shelf of home tests. The bubble stone's provenance is likewise unrecorded — Robert's account of it says pieces like his wash up on shores and are beachcombed and polished, but his own piece's road to his hands is not yet written down.
*What would settle it:* Robert himself — his memory, any receipt, listing, or message that survives from the purchase or the gift. One paragraph from the man who holds the stone would do more than any instrument on this list.

**5. What are its exact dimensions and weight?**
Nearly known, and the "nearly" is the honest part. The studies measured Gemini from photographs against a ruler in frame: about 6.6 cm long, about 2.4 cm at its widest, the bubble stone about 30 by 24 mm with a dome about 13 mm high. But Gemini's thickness — read off edge-on silhouettes — stands at about 1.0 cm, plus or minus two millimetres, and is labelled in the study as the softest number in the document. Neither piece has been weighed. Without a weight, the specific gravity cannot be computed, and specific gravity is one of the few home measurements with real teeth (amber lands at about 1.05–1.10).
*What would settle it:* sixty seconds — a caliper on the thickness, a kitchen scale under each piece. The desk's softest numbers become facts the day Robert chooses to take them.

**6. Are the two pieces kin?**
Gemini and the bubble stone sit on the same table and share a colour family and a polish, and one of Robert's frames shows them together. Whether they are the same material, from the same deposit — or two strangers who met in a shop — nothing on record says.
*What would settle it:* the same road as Questions 1 and 4 — provenance for both, or one laboratory session with both pieces in hand, fingerprints compared.

## II. The making of the piece

**7. How many times did the resin flow?**
The stone is banded through and through — sweeping flow lines and cloudy planes, the layered signature of resin that moved, rested, and moved again. Gemini was not made in one pour; it is a stack of episodes. But the photographs cannot count the episodes: some bands are bold, some are ghost-faint, some planes are fractures rather than flow surfaces, and no reader at a photograph can honestly separate them into a number.
*What would settle it:* partly, perhaps — examination of the piece in hand under magnification, or a micro-CT scan (Question 21) reading its internal layering in three dimensions, might count major flow surfaces. A confident exact count may simply not survive in the stone, and the honest answer might always be "several — more than two, fewer than anyone could swear to."

**8. Over how long?**
If the flows can barely be counted, their calendar is further still from reach. Successive resin flows on one tree might be separated by hours, by seasons, or by years. Jiminy and the spider lie in the same small region of the stone; whether they arrived within one afternoon or across a decade of the tree's bleeding, the photographs do not say (Question 19 carries the pair's share of this).
*What would settle it:* very likely nothing, ever, in full. If the creatures could be shown to lie on the same flow surface (Question 19), the honest statement would narrow to "the same flow, or flows close in time." The width of that window would remain the tree's secret.

**9. Why did the tree bleed so much?**
Resin is a tree's bandage — it pours over wounds: insect borings, storm tears, cracked bark, disease. A piece this generous with resin came from a tree under some kind of pressure, or from a species that bleeds freely at a touch, and the record cannot choose between those or name the wound. There is a bleak poetry the record *does* support: the forest's injuries are the only reason its portrait survives.
*What would settle it:* nothing available to us. The wound itself is not in the piece — only its bandage is. This unknown is recorded because it is real, and because an honest map marks the doors that do not open as carefully as the ones that do.

## III. Jiminy

**10. What species is she — or even what genus?**
Not approachable from photographs, and the study does not attempt it. What the frames honestly carry is a ladder with three rungs: order Orthoptera (grasshoppers and crickets) is supported by the jumping legs and wing covers; suborder Ensifera — the long-feelered half — is the firmest rung, because her antennae run longer than her body, which no grasshopper's ever do; family Gryllidae, the true crickets, is *consistent* with everything visible but not confirmed, because the characters that key a cricket to her family live below the frames' reach, and small neighbouring families cannot all be excluded at this size.
*What would settle it:* the piece in an entomologist's hands, under a microscope. Amber crickets are keyed on fine structures — the hearing organ on the front shin, the foot-segment count, the wing venation, the ovipositor's valves — and even then, some amber specimens end their examination at family. Genus and species would be a gift, not an expectation.

**11. Is Jiminy a "her"?**
Robert named her Jiminy and calls her "her," and the name rides regardless — it is his. But as a reading of the stone, her sex is **indeterminate**, and the study hunted the question deliberately before saying so. A female cricket carries an ovipositor — an egg-laying tube at the rear, usually a slim sword that sticks out past the wing tips where nothing can hide it. The desk cropped the rear tip tight from three angles and read the side profile besides: *no ovipositor is visible in any frame.* The wing covers end in one clean point, and nothing projects past them. That makes "her" unsupported by the photographs — not disproven: a short ovipositor can hide beneath folded wings from every angle shot, a cloudy flow plane sits near the tip, and a late nymph of either sex carries none at all. Symmetrically, there is no positive evidence of male either: the song apparatus on a male's wing cover is below the frames' resolution.
*What would settle it:* two roads. Aimed photographs first — the rear tip lit from behind, tight, on Robert's word, following the field protocol the study appends. And finally, a microscope: the same entomologist's look that settles Question 10 would very likely settle this one in the same sitting.

**12. Was she grown?**
The wing covers reach the body's tip, which leans adult — but leaning is all it does. If she is a late nymph, Question 11's missing ovipositor explains itself, and the whole portrait shifts a little: not a mother-aged cricket but a young one, most of her life unlived when the resin took her.
*What would settle it:* the microscope again — wing development and venation are nymph-versus-adult characters at this size. No photograph in the record resolves them.

**13. What is the fine print of her body?**
A small sheaf of parts the frames gesture at and cannot confirm: her antennae trace for at least 15 millimetres — longer than her body — and then fade into glare and flow planes, so their true length is unrecoverable; her cerci, the paired sensory spikes at the rear, are suggested but cannot be separated from the wing tip; the two neat rows of spines a living cricket carries along her shin show at most a hint of serration at the limit of resolution; her three tiny simple eyes are below resolution entirely; the hearing organ that would help key her family (Question 10) has never been seen, because no frame was ever aimed at the front shin.
*What would settle it:* aimed frames — the front shin and the rear tip, backlit, per the study's protocol — and beyond them, the microscope. Some of it (the antennae's lost ends, dissolved in flow planes) the stone may keep no matter the instrument.

**14. Was she alive when the resin took her?**
The deepest question about Jiminy, and the one the record holds most carefully. Resin takes the living and the dead alike: a cricket walking the trunk can step in and be held, and a dead cricket can be blown or washed onto a flow and sealed without ever struggling. Her posture — whole, naturally arranged, legs folded in the ordinary carriage of her kind, antennae swept out — is consistent with a living capture, and consistent with a quiet delivery of a body. Nothing in any frame shows a struggle frozen mid-motion, and nothing shows the shrivelling of a long-dead husk.
*What would settle it:* honestly, perhaps nothing conclusively. A microscope might read fine evidence — the state of the body's tissues as the resin modelled them, trapped air at the mouthparts — and tip the balance toward a lean. But "lean" is likely the ceiling. Her last minute is hers; the stone kept her form, not her story, and this essay marks the door as one that probably does not open — which is itself a finding worth printing.

**15. The question everyone asks first — is there anything of her left inside the form?**
This one is not an unknown, and it belongs on the map precisely because it has an answer, and the answer closes a door. What amber keeps is form: the body's shape modelled in resin, the soft substance gone. The famous 1990s claims of DNA recovered from amber insects failed replication, every one; and in 2013 Penney and colleagues could recover no DNA even from subfossil copal insects only thousands of years old — a blink beside amber's span. There is nothing in Gemini to drill for. What Robert holds is her shape, her posture, her measurements — her portrait, not her molecules. The door is closed, and saying so plainly is part of the map.

## IV. The spider

**16. What family is she?**
She is the second pass's triumph and its limit in one. The closer frames settled what she *is* — a small spider, supported at photo tier: at least six legs individually countable, seven leg shafts traced, all long, thin, and many-jointed, drawn in against a body of about four millimetres. The alternatives were weighed on the record: mite argued against (her legs are too long, her body suggests two parts rather than one fused sac), pseudoscorpion argued against (no pincers anywhere), harvestman argued against but not wholly excludable. But her family lives in parts no frame resolves — her eye pattern, her spinnerets, the form of her pedipalps — and family and genus are not attempted.
*What would settle it:* a microscope on the piece, and an arachnologist's eye. Spiders in amber are keyed exactly on those small parts, and four millimetres is a workable size — of all the unknowns in this essay, this is one of the most genuinely settleable.

**17. Is the spider a "she"?**
Nobody has claimed it, and the record doesn't: her sex rides entirely on the pedipalps — swollen in a male, slim in a female — and the pedipalps are below resolution.
*What would settle it:* the same microscope session as Question 16. One look, two answers.

**18. How did she come to be there?**
A spider on a resinous trunk is no accident of scenery: spiders hunt where small things walk, and a trunk's resin flows are where small things get caught — living larders. Whether she was hunting the flow's edge when it took her, walking the bark on other business, or arrived dead, the frames cannot say. Her legs are flexed and drawn in — the posture trapped arthropods commonly show — which suits a living capture without proving one.
*What would settle it:* her posture under a microscope might strengthen or soften the living-capture reading; the *reason* she was there — hunting, passing, fleeing — is behaviour, and behaviour leaves no fossil. Door marked: opens a crack, no further.

## V. The pair

**19. Did the same flow take them both?**
They are, in the scientists' word, **syninclusions** — multiple inclusions sharing one piece. One frame (35429) holds them together, and the desk measured it: nearest edges about six to seven millimetres apart in projection, centres about twelve, with **no contact visible in any frame** — proximity, not interaction. But the slab is about a centimetre thick, the two may lie at different depths, and the flow that sealed one need not be the flow that sealed the other. Adjacent pages of the stone's stack are not necessarily the same page.
*What would settle it:* a micro-CT scan could place both creatures in three dimensions against the flow surfaces around them — if each lies on the same surface, the same flow took them, and if not, not. That is a real, non-destructive test, and it is the only one on this map that speaks to the pair directly.

**20. Did they ever meet?**
No frame shows either creature oriented toward, touching, or engaged with the other. A centimetre of resin — an ocean, at their scale — lies between them, and a spider of four millimetres and a cricket of eleven do not, in any case, keep company in life: to each other they would have been, at most, weather. Whether their living paths ever crossed on that trunk is a question the stone does not merely decline to answer; it contains no machinery for answering it.
*What would settle it:* nothing. This unknown is permanent, and the map marks it so that no future telling — including the desk's own — ever paints an encounter as found fact. They are neighbours in the record. Neighbours is the whole of it, and it is enough.

**21. How far apart are they, truly?**
The projected separation is measured (≈6–7 mm edge to edge); the true separation is at least that, because depth is unknown and the slab's centimetre of thickness could hold most of a centimetre of it between them. Every measurement in both studies carries this same shadow: photographs flatten a world that has depth, and sizes and distances read through curved, clouded resin are apparent, not final.
*What would settle it:* micro-CT — the same scan as Question 19 — which reads the piece in three dimensions and turns every projected figure in the record into a placed one.

## VI. The menagerie — the specks

**22. What are the three that almost have names?**
Beyond Jiminy and the spider, the census graded three entries *possible arthropod*, four forms among them: two elongated teardrop bodies, about four millimetres each, side by side near the top of one frame — fly-shaped through the cloudy resin, with a seed or debris fragment as the standing alternative; one clear dark body, about three millimetres, with a pale cross-division across it — the most organised of the small candidates; and one ant-shaped form, about two and a half to three millimetres, with a banded projection and radiating lines for legs — ant-*shaped*, the census insists, since an ant needs three body parts and elbowed antennae and neither resolves.
*What would settle them:* aimed, backlit, magnifier frames on each, following the study's protocol — and past photography's best, the microscope. These three sit exactly at the border where better pictures could still win.

**23. What is the round black spot?**
The single most intriguing unnamed thing in the stone: a clean, round, sharp-edged, wholly opaque spot of about two millimetres, seen from *both faces* of the piece — which proves it sits inside the stone and is no surface mark. No appendages, no internal structure, no hint of organisation. A small body seen end-on, a seed, and a filled bubble all remain open, and the photographs cannot rank them.
*What would settle it:* magnification first — a lens pass might find an edge character, a seam, a stalk, any asymmetry that argues body against bubble. Micro-CT could read its density and shape in three dimensions. If both fail, it keeps its mystery honestly; the census has already given it the only name it has earned: the round black spot.

**24. What are the other thirty-six — and the uncounted?**
Under the three near-names, the census holds thirty-six entries graded *indeterminate* — smudges, ovals, comma-specks, dot-pairs, shards — plus dozens of sub-millimetre specks recorded as an uncounted field, present and unnamed. Somewhere in that field, very plausibly, ride the smallest citizens of all: pollen grains and spores, the microfossils that in laboratory hands can name a forest's climate and kind (Question 3). The census's discipline is the point of it: the entry count is an upper bound, not a headcount, because the frames overlap and photograph the same zones again and again from both faces.
*What would settle them:* not photographs — microscopy, and specimen-grade microscopy at that. Pollen is identified grain by grain under high magnification by specialists; that work exists in the world, on pieces whose provenance earns it a laboratory's time (Question 4 again). The specks' road runs through every other door on this map.

**25. Are there ants in there, as Robert reports?**
Robert's own sightings stand in the study as an addendum, at his tier, credited to him: he reports ants and other very small bugs — "widgets," in his word — seen by his own eyes in the stone, too small for him to describe confidently and hard to photograph with his present method. The desk's census neither confirms his report nor is confirmed by it; the spider began her record as exactly such a speck, and was promoted only when closer frames earned it. His ants stand tonight where she stood this morning.
*What would settle it:* his next frames, shot by the protocol the study appends — backlight through the stone, tap-focus, low ISO, no pinch-zoom, the magnifying glass used as a macro lens, everything braced, several frames per angle. The desk reads whatever arrives and promotes only what the frames earn. The man who found a spider in a speck has earned the presumption that his eye is worth aiming.

## VII. The sealed room — the bubble stone

**26. How big is the bubble, truly?**
The second stone holds a hollow cavity that shows through its dome as a pale disc about 10–12 millimetres across — *as it appears*. The dome is a lens and magnifies; the cavity's true width is smaller by an unknown factor, plausibly seven to ten millimetres — an estimate, not a measurement. Even at the low end, it is a room where amber usually keeps closets: most bubbles in amber are pinheads, and this one spans roughly forty percent of its piece's length.
*What would settle it:* micro-CT, which measures the cavity itself instead of its magnified portrait — the same scan that answers Questions 19, 21, and 27.

**27. Is the bubble truly sealed?**
Robert reports it has not breached the surface, and the frames support him: in every readable frame the polish over and around the cavity is smooth and continuous, the flanks unbroken, and the one internal line running down from the disc fades out inside the stone. One frame was too blurred to test anything and is recorded as *untested*, not passed. What photographs cannot exclude is a hairline crack finer than their resolution — and the seal is the load-bearing wall of the whole ancient-air question (Question 30), so the gap is worth naming exactly.
*What would settle it:* an in-hand examination under magnification, and definitively, micro-CT — a channel either reaches the surface in three dimensions or it does not.

**28. What is the dark mass inside the bubble?**
Something is in there — that much is settled, and by the strongest evidence in either study. The mass was formerly stuck to the cavity's wall; it came loose (Question 29), and when Robert tipped the piece, it *moved and rolled* — first-hand manipulation evidence that places it inside the cavity, free, in a void, beyond photographic argument. What it *is*, no evidence yet touches. The candidates stand as the study left them: plant debris is the commonest solid inclusion in amber and fits the portrait; a tiny insect or a fragment of one, at its true size of roughly a millimetre and a half, cannot be excluded and cannot be shown; a dark gob of the resin's own making, folded loose, is possible; a mineral grain is possible. Its quick fall and free roll are consistent with a gas-filled cavity — a loose object in liquid would sink and drift, cushioned — but that is an inference from behaviour, labelled as one, not a measurement.
*What would settle it:* micro-CT might read its shape and density — organic debris, insect, and resin gob do not always differ enough in density to separate, and the study says so plainly. Beyond imaging, nothing non-destructive identifies a millimetre of dark matter through a centimetre of stone. Its identity may be a permanent unknown wearing a temporary label — the map records the possibility without flinching.

**29. What set the mass free?**
It was stuck; then, one day, it was lying loose. Robert's own suggestion is temperature change — recorded in the study as his hypothesis, not a finding, and carried here the same way. Thermal expansion works on stone and speck at different rates, and a Kansas year gives a windowsill piece heat and cold in full measure, so the hypothesis is physically respectable. It is also untestable after the fact: the event happened unobserved, some day between one looking and the next.
*What would settle it:* nothing available. A small door, marked honestly as closed — and a reminder that even the stones Robert holds daily keep a private life between his lookings.

**30. What gas is in the bubble today?**
Unknown — no instrument has read it. The one method that could report the cavity's present contents without breaking the stone is Raman spectroscopy, a laser fingerprint-reader that has measured nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and methane inside single inclusions in glass and minerals. Its cautions ride with it: gas signals are weak, and amber fluoresces under laser light in a way that can swamp them. The classic method — crushing the piece under vacuum and mass-reading the released gas — would answer fully, including isotope ratios, and would destroy the bubble by definition. It is the door that cannot be unopened, and it stands last on every honest list.
*What would settle it:* a Raman session at a laboratory equipped for single-inclusion work — attempted in the knowledge that fluorescence may refuse the reading, and that the answer, if won, describes the gas as it is *now*.

**31. Is the gas ancient air?**
This is the question the second stone was bought, in Robert's hope, to carry — he remembers the famous studies, and the study's appendix walks the whole literature. The honest state of it: in 1988 Berner and Landis reported in *Science* that gas crushed from amber bubbles was substantially original ancient air, and read from it a Late Cretaceous atmosphere of more than thirty percent oxygen against today's twenty-one. Within months, the counter-attack landed in the same journal and it went to the foundation: Hopfenberg's group measured gas movement through amber directly and found it permeable — amber absorbs and transports gases, even molecules larger than oxygen; Horibe and Craig's experiments pointed the same way; Beck added that the resin itself reacts with enclosed oxygen, reworking a bubble's contents from its own walls. The field's burden shifted then and has never shifted back: oxygen history is now written from geochemical models and the fossil-charcoal record, not from amber; Landis's 2018 return, arguing amber gas "may represent, at least in part, ancient air," stands as a defended minority dissent. The claim is **contested**, and the contest is not close to even.
*What would settle it:* for this specimen, nothing — and the map must say why, because it is the deepest fence in the essay. Even a perfect Raman reading (Question 30) reports today's contents; the diffusion objection is that today's contents cannot be *proven* ancient, because the physics of the material says they should have exchanged and been reworked across the years. Provenance and FTIR (Questions 1–4) come first in any case: if the piece proved to be copal, its air is young air and the question closes kindly. If it proved true amber, its bubble would enter the literature — if it entered at all — as a contested reading, not a measurement of the ancient sky. Robert's hope of contributing the piece to science is recorded in the study with respect and answered with a map; the map's last road is longer than the 1988 headlines promised, and its final step costs the bubble itself.

## The use of a map of ignorance

Thirty-one questions, and the honest reader will notice how few of them the desk expects to close from a desk chair. One closes with a paragraph of Robert's memory. Two or three close with a caliper, a scale, a salt solution, an ultraviolet torch. A handful close in a single afternoon with a microscope — and those are the spider's name, Jiminy's sex, the near-named specks. The deepest — the flows' calendar, the creatures' last minutes, the age of the air in the sealed room — do not close at all, and the map says so in the same ink as the rest.

That is not a failure of the piece or of the looking. It is the piece's true shape, drawn at last. People write about what they know because knowledge photographs well; but a specimen like this one is mostly questions wearing a thin coat of answers, and nobody had drawn *that* picture. Now it is drawn. Robert has a phrase for what such a map is for — he speaks of slipping into his **mind palace** and looking around: a picture of the unknowns, laid out room by room, that a mind can walk through and feel the size of. Consider this essay the floor plan. The stone sits in Hays, in one man's keeping, holding a cricket, a spider, a crowd of the unnamed, and a room of sealed air — and everything it refuses to tell us is now written down in one place, numbered, with the road to each answer marked where a road exists, and the wall marked honestly where none does.

The questions are not the opposite of the wonder. They are the wonder, itemised.
